# Probability of Opening Mail: A Normal Approximation Problem

## Introduction

A seller of printed advertisements believes that 56% of adults usually open all the mail they receive. We want to know what happens in a random sample of 1000 adults if that figure is still correct. Specifically, we need two probabilities:

a) that fewer than 541 adults in the sample open all their mail, and
b) that 570 or more do.

Each adult either opens all their mail or does not, and the adults are chosen at random. So the number who do, X, follows a binomial distribution with n = 1000 and p = 0.56. Calculating binomial probabilities for hundreds of values by hand is impractical, so we use the normal distribution as an approximation.

## Step 1: Checking that the approximation is reasonable

The normal approximation works well when both np and n(1 − p) are at least 5 (some texts ask for 10).

- np = 1000 × 0.56 = 560
- n(1 − p) = 1000 × 0.44 = 440

Both are far above 5, so the binomial distribution is close to a bell curve here and the approximation is safe.

## Step 2: Mean and standard deviation

For a binomial distribution:

- Mean: μ = np = 560
- Variance: σ² = np(1 − p) = 1000 × 0.56 × 0.44 = 246.4
- Standard deviation: σ = √246.4 ≈ 15.70

So in a sample of 1000, we expect about 560 people to open all their mail, and results usually land within roughly 16 people of that figure.

## Step 3: A note on the continuity correction

X can only be a whole number (540, 541, 542, and so on), but the normal curve is continuous. To account for this, we treat each whole number as covering the interval from half a unit below it to half a unit above it. For example, the value 541 covers the range 540.5 to 541.5. This adjustment is called the continuity correction, and it makes the approximation more accurate.

## Part (a): P(X < 541)

Since X is a whole number, "less than 541" means 540 or fewer. The value 540 extends up to 540.5 on the continuous scale, so:

P(X < 541) = P(X ≤ 540) ≈ P(Y < 540.5), where Y is normal with mean 560 and standard deviation 15.70.

Convert to a z-score:

z = (540.5 − 560) / 15.70 = −19.5 / 15.70 ≈ −1.24

From the standard normal table, the area to the left of z = −1.24 is about 0.1075. Using the unrounded z of −1.2423 gives about 0.1071.

**P(X < 541) ≈ 0.107**

This means that if 56% is the true rate, there is roughly a 10.7% chance that fewer than 541 people in the sample would open all their mail.

## Part (b): P(X ≥ 570)

"570 or more" begins at 570, which on the continuous scale starts at 569.5. So:

P(X ≥ 570) ≈ P(Y > 569.5)

z = (569.5 − 560) / 15.70 = 9.5 / 15.70 ≈ 0.61

The table gives the area to the left of z = 0.61 as about 0.7291. We want the area to the right, so we subtract from 1:

P(Y > 569.5) = 1 − 0.7291 = 0.2709

Using the unrounded z of 0.6052 gives about 0.2725.

**P(X ≥ 570) ≈ 0.27**

So there is roughly a 27% chance that 570 or more people would open all their mail.

## Checking the effect of the continuity correction

To see how much the correction matters, here are the answers without it:

- (a) z = (541 − 560)/15.70 = −1.21, giving about 0.113
- (b) z = (570 − 560)/15.70 = 0.64, giving about 0.262

These differ from the corrected answers by about 0.005 to 0.01. That is a small difference, but the corrected values are closer to the true binomial probabilities, so they are the ones to report.

## Conclusion

If 56% of adults usually open all their mail, then in a random sample of 1000 adults:

- The probability that fewer than 541 open all their mail is about 0.107, or 10.7%.
- The probability that 570 or more open all their mail is about 0.27, or 27%.

Both results make sense given the expected count of 560. Getting 570 is only about 0.6 standard deviations above the mean, so it happens fairly often. Getting 540 or fewer is about 1.2 standard deviations below the mean, which is less common but still not unusual. Neither outcome would give much reason to doubt the seller's 56% estimate. A result far from 560, say below 520 or above 600, would be a stronger sign that the real rate had changed.
