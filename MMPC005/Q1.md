# Primary and Secondary Data, and Methods of Collecting Primary Data

## Introduction

Every study, whether a market survey, a research project or a business decision, begins with data. Before collecting anything, the researcher must decide whether to gather fresh information or to use information that already exists. This is the difference between primary and secondary data. This assignment explains the difference between the two, describes the main methods of collecting primary data and indicates when each method suits best.

## Primary and Secondary Data

**Primary data** is information collected for the first time by the researcher, directly from the source, for the specific purpose of the study at hand. A company that interviews its own customers to learn why sales fell is collecting primary data.

**Secondary data** is information that was already collected by someone else for another purpose and is now being used again. Government census reports, company annual reports, published journals, newspapers and industry reports are examples.

| Basis | Primary Data | Secondary Data |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Collected first-hand from respondents or the field | Taken from existing records, books or reports |
| Purpose | Collected for the present study | Collected earlier for some other purpose |
| Originality | Original and fresh | Already processed, so it is second-hand |
| Cost | High | Low |
| Time | Takes long to collect | Quick to obtain |
| Fit with the problem | Matches the researcher's exact needs | May not fit the problem exactly |
| Control over quality | Researcher controls how it is gathered | Researcher cannot check how it was gathered |
| Reliability | Reliable if the method is sound | Depends on the source and may be outdated or biased |
| Effort | Needs planning, staff and skill | Needs searching and checking |
| Examples | Survey results, interview records, experiment readings | Census data, published reports, past sales records |

Neither is better in every case. Secondary data is usually checked first because it is cheaper and faster, and it helps to define the problem. Primary data is collected when the existing information is missing, outdated or unsuitable.

## Methods of Collecting Primary Data

### 1. Observation

The researcher watches and records behaviour or events directly, without asking questions. It may be done openly or without the subjects knowing, and the observer may take part in the activity or stay apart. Records can be kept by notes, checklists, cameras or counters.

**Example:** Watching how customers move through a store, which shelves they stop at and how long they wait at the billing counter.

**Merits:** It records what people actually do, not what they say they do. It does not depend on people's willingness to answer. It is useful for those who cannot speak, such as children.

**Limits:** It shows behaviour but not the reasons behind it. It can be slow and costly. The observer's own bias may affect the record, and people who know they are watched may act differently.

**Use it when:** the behaviour itself is the subject of study, when people may not be able or willing to describe their actions accurately, or when the study concerns events that can be seen, such as traffic flow, shopper movement, machine use or employee working methods.

### 2. Personal Interview

The researcher meets the respondent face to face and asks questions. It may be **structured**, with fixed questions in a fixed order, or **unstructured**, where the conversation flows freely around a topic. A semi-structured form uses a list of main questions with freedom to ask follow-ups.

**Example:** Asking a manager in detail why she resigned, or interviewing farmers about how they decide which crop to plant.

**Merits:** It gives deep and detailed information. The interviewer can explain unclear questions, notice expressions and ask follow-up questions. Response rates are usually high.

**Limits:** It is expensive and time-consuming, especially when respondents are spread out. Results depend on the skill of the interviewer, and the interviewer's attitude can influence answers. Comparing unstructured interviews is difficult.

**Use it when:** detailed, personal or complex information is needed, the topic is sensitive, the number of respondents is small, or the researcher wants to understand reasons and attitudes. It suits exploratory studies.

### 3. Telephone Interview

Questions are asked over the phone instead of in person.

**Merits:** It is quicker and cheaper than personal interviews. It reaches people over a wide area. Results can be collected fast, and it is easier to supervise.

**Limits:** Only people with phones can be reached. The conversation must be short, and respondents can end the call or refuse to answer. The interviewer cannot see facial expressions or show materials. Many people do not like unexpected calls.

**Use it when:** the questions are short and simple, the answers are needed quickly, respondents are spread across many places or when the respondents have already agreed to be contacted, for instance in a customer follow-up after a service visit.

### 4. Questionnaire (Mailed or Online)

A written set of questions is sent to respondents, who fill it in on their own and return it. It is sent by post, email or through online forms. The questions may be closed (with fixed options) or open (written answers).

**Example:** An online form sent to all employees to rate job satisfaction.

**Merits:** It is low in cost and can reach a large number of people over a wide area. Respondents answer in their own time, which allows thought, and there is no interviewer bias. Anonymous forms can bring honest answers on sensitive matters.

**Limits:** The response rate is often low, and those who reply may not represent everyone. Respondents must be literate and understand the questions. Nobody is available to clarify doubts, and answers cannot be probed. Some questions may be left blank. Careful design is essential, since a badly worded question cannot be corrected later.

**Use it when:** the respondents are many and spread out, questions are simple and clear, respondents are educated and have access to the medium, and the topic is such that people may answer more honestly in private.

### 5. Schedule

A schedule is also a set of questions, but it is filled in by a trained enumerator who asks the questions and records the answers. It is not left to respondents to fill in themselves.

**Example:** Census workers visiting households with a form, or a field survey among rural families where many cannot read.

**Merits:** It can be used with illiterate respondents. The enumerator can explain questions and make sure that all are answered. The response rate is high, and the information is more complete.

**Limits:** It costs more than a mailed questionnaire because enumerators must be hired and trained. It takes longer. The enumerator's bias or errors can affect the data.

**Use it when:** the respondents cannot read or write well, the survey is large but accuracy and completeness matter and a trained team is available. It suits household and rural surveys.

*Note:* A questionnaire is filled in by the respondent, while a schedule is filled in by the interviewer. This is the main difference between the two.

### 6. Focus Group Discussion

A small group of about six to ten people, with a moderator, discusses a topic together for an hour or two. The moderator guides the conversation and records the views and reactions.

**Example:** A company showing a new product design to a group of consumers and hearing what they like, dislike and suggest.

**Merits:** It brings out a wide range of opinions quickly, and the group conversation can produce ideas that a single interview would not. It shows how people influence one another and gives a good feel for attitudes and language.

**Limits:** The group is small and not statistically representative. Strong personalities may dominate and quiet members may hold back. The results are hard to measure and depend heavily on the moderator.

**Use it when:** the researcher is at an early stage and wants ideas or hypotheses, is testing a new product, advertisement or concept, or wants to understand opinions and feelings, not to count them.

### 7. Experimentation

The researcher changes one factor under controlled conditions and observes the effect on another, while keeping other things the same. Often two groups are compared, one exposed to the change and one not.

**Example:** Selling the same product at two different prices in two similar stores to see how sales respond, or trying a new training method with one team and comparing its output with a similar team that did not receive it.

**Merits:** It is the strongest way of showing cause and effect. Conditions are controlled, so the result is clearer.

**Limits:** It can be expensive and slow. It is hard to control every outside factor in real situations. People may behave differently because they know they are part of a test. There may also be ethical limits in experimenting on people.

**Use it when:** the aim is to find out whether one thing actually causes another, for example the effect of price, packaging, advertising or a new process, and where the situation can be controlled well enough.

### 8. Case Study

An in-depth study of a single person, organisation, event or situation, using several sources such as interviews, documents and observation.

**Example:** A detailed study of why one particular firm succeeded in a market where others failed.

**Merits:** It gives rich and detailed understanding of how and why something happened and reveals factors that a survey might miss.

**Limits:** Findings relate to one case and cannot be easily generalised. It takes a lot of time and depends on the researcher's judgement.

**Use it when:** a deep understanding of a particular case is needed, when the subject is complex, or when the researcher wants to generate ideas for later testing on a larger scale.

## Choosing Among the Methods: A Summary

| Method | Best suited for |
|---|---|
| Observation | Studying actual behaviour that can be seen, such as shopper movement |
| Personal interview | Detailed, sensitive or complex information from a few people |
| Telephone interview | Quick, short surveys over a wide area |
| Questionnaire | Large, scattered, literate respondents; simple questions |
| Schedule | Large surveys among respondents who cannot read or write easily |
| Focus group | Early-stage ideas, testing concepts and understanding opinions |
| Experiment | Proving cause and effect |
| Case study | In-depth understanding of one complex situation |

**Factors to consider when choosing.**
- **The objective:** whether the study needs numbers across many people or deep understanding of a few.
- **The nature of respondents:** their education, location and willingness to answer.
- **Time and budget:** interviews and experiments cost more and take longer than questionnaires and phone surveys.
- **Accuracy needed:** a decision with high stakes warrants a more careful method.
- **Sensitivity of the topic:** private matters may need anonymity or a trusted interviewer.

Often, more than one method is combined. For example, a researcher might hold focus groups to find out what issues matter and then use a questionnaire to measure how widely they are felt.

## Conclusion

Primary data is collected fresh for the purpose at hand, while secondary data was collected earlier by someone else. Primary data fits the problem better and is more current but costs more and takes longer. The main methods of collecting it are observation, personal interviews, telephone interviews, questionnaires, schedules, focus groups, experiments and case studies. No method is best in every case. The right choice depends on what is to be found out, who the respondents are, how much time and money are available and how accurate the results must be. A careful researcher matches the method to the situation and, where possible, combines methods to check one against another.
