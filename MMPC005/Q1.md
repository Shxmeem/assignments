# Primary and Secondary Data and the Methods of Collecting Primary Data

## 1. Introduction

Every study begins with **data**. Data means the facts and figures a researcher collects to answer a question. Where the data comes from decides how accurate it is, how much it costs, and how well it fits the problem. Data is divided into two kinds:

- **Primary data**
- **Secondary data**

This assignment explains the difference between them and describes the main ways of collecting primary data and when to use each.

---

## 2. Primary Data

**Primary data** is data collected for the first time by the researcher, for the problem being studied. Nobody has gathered it before.

> **Example:** A shopkeeper who asks fifty customers why they stopped buying a certain soap is collecting primary data.

---

## 3. Secondary Data

**Secondary data** is data that already exists. Someone else collected it for some other purpose, and the researcher uses it again.

> **Examples:** Census reports, company annual reports, newspapers and research journals.

---

## 4. Difference Between the Two

| Basis | Primary Data | Secondary Data |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Comes straight from the people or events being studied. | Comes from records and reports that were made earlier. |
| **Purpose** | Gathered to suit the exact question of the researcher. | Gathered for another question, so it may fit only in part. |
| **Cost and Time** | Takes more money, effort and time because the researcher has to plan the work, reach people and record answers. | Cheap and quick to get, since it only has to be found and read. |
| **Quality Control** | The researcher knows how it was collected. | The researcher often cannot tell how careful the collection was, and the data may be old. |

> **In practice:** Researchers often look at secondary data first. If it answers the question, no further collection is needed. If it does not, they collect primary data.

---

## 5. Methods of Collecting Primary Data

### 5.1 Observation

In this method the researcher watches people or events and records what happens, **without asking questions**.

> **Example:** A store manager may watch which shelves customers stop at and how long they stay.

**When to use it:** Observation suits situations where people cannot or will not describe their behaviour correctly, such as children at play or shoppers in a busy market.

**Weakness:** It shows what people do but not why, and people may act differently when they know they are watched.

---

### 5.2 Personal Interview

Here the researcher meets a person **face to face** and asks questions, either from a fixed list or in a free conversation. The researcher can explain a question and ask follow-up questions.

**When to use it:** Use it when the topic is complex or personal, when the number of people is small, and when detailed answers are needed.

**Weakness:** It takes a lot of time and money, so it is not good for very large groups.

---

### 5.3 Telephone Interview

The researcher asks questions **over the phone**. It is faster and cheaper than meeting people in person.

**When to use it:** Use it when the questions are short and simple, and the answers are needed quickly.

> **Example:** A company checking customer satisfaction after a delivery can use it.

**Weakness:** It is not suitable for long or sensitive questions, and people without phones cannot be reached.

---

### 5.4 Questionnaire

A questionnaire is a **printed or online set of questions** that people fill in themselves. It may be sent by post, email or handed out in person.

**When to use it:** Use it when many people spread over a wide area must be reached at low cost, and when the questions are clear and do not need explaining.

**Weakness:** Many people do not return it, and those who do may not answer carefully.

---

### 5.5 Survey by Sampling

A survey collects information from a chosen group of people, called a **sample**, who stand for the whole population.

**When to use it:** Use it when the population is too big to study fully, such as all the households in a state.

**Weakness:** A well chosen sample gives a fair picture at lower cost, but a badly chosen one misleads.

---

### 5.6 Experiment

In an experiment the researcher **changes one factor on purpose** and watches the effect on another, while keeping other things the same.

> **Example:** A company may sell the same biscuit in two kinds of packets in two similar towns to see which one sells more.

**When to use it:** Use it when the aim is to find out **cause and effect**, that is, whether one thing really leads to another.

**Weakness:** It gives strong evidence but is costly and hard to run in real life, because it is difficult to keep all other conditions equal.

---

### 5.7 Focus Group

A small group of **six to ten people** is brought together to talk about a topic while a leader guides the talk and notes the views that come up.

**When to use it:** Use it when the researcher wants to understand opinions, feelings and reasons, for instance before launching a new product.

**Weakness:** The results cannot be applied to a whole population, because the group is small and a strong speaker may influence the rest.

---

## 6. Choosing the Right Method

No single method is best in every case. The choice depends on:

- the **purpose** of the study,
- the **time and money** available, and
- the **number of people** to be reached.

| Aim | Best Method |
|---|---|
| Deep understanding | Interviews and focus groups |
| Facts from many people | Questionnaire or sample survey |
| Prove cause and effect | Experiment |

> Many studies use **two or more methods together**.

---

## 7. Conclusion

- **Primary data** is collected fresh by the researcher and fits the problem closely, but it costs more time and money.
- **Secondary data** is already available and cheap, but it may not match the problem exactly.

The main methods of collecting primary data are:

1. Observation
2. Personal interview
3. Telephone interview
4. Questionnaire
5. Sample survey
6. Experiment
7. Focus group

Each has its own strengths and limits, and a researcher should pick the one that suits the **question**, the **people** and the **resources** at hand.
