**Introduction**

Whenever a researcher wants to learn something about a group, such as the monthly spending of households in a city or the opinions of students in a university, the first question is whom to ask. The complete group under study is called the population, and checking every member of it is called a census. A census is sometimes possible, but in most real situations it is not practical. For this reason researchers select a smaller part of the population, called a sample, study it carefully, and use what they find to say something about the whole group. This assignment lists the reasons why sampling is so widely used and then explains the main difference between probability and non-probability sampling.

**Reasons Why Sampling Is Attractive**

1. Lower cost. Contacting every member of a large population means paying for travel, printing, staff and data entry for thousands or even millions of people. A sample needs only a fraction of that money. A company testing a new tea blend can survey 500 customers instead of every buyer in the country.

2. Saves time. A census can take months or years. By the time results are ready, the situation may have changed. A sample can be collected and analysed in days, which matters when a decision is needed soon, such as before a product launch or an election.

3. Better quality of work. With fewer respondents, a team can train its interviewers properly, supervise them, check forms and follow up on missing answers. In a census the sheer volume often leads to rushed work and careless mistakes. A small, carefully handled sample can therefore give more reliable information than a poorly managed full count.

4. Testing that destroys the item. Some studies make a census impossible. A factory that wants to know how long its bulbs last, or how much force a batch of steel cables can bear, has to use the product until it fails. Testing every item would leave nothing to sell. Only a sample can be tested.

5. Populations that cannot be fully reached. Some populations are infinite or constantly changing, such as fish in a river, or all the customers who may ever buy a product. Others are spread over areas that are hard to travel to. Listing and meeting every member is simply not possible.

6. Easier handling of data. A small data set is simpler to record, clean and analyse. Fewer errors creep in during entry, and results come out faster.

7. Accuracy can be measured. When a sample is chosen properly, statistical theory lets the researcher estimate how far the result may be from the true value, through measures such as standard error and confidence intervals. The reader of the report then knows how much trust to place in the figures.

8. Wider scope. Because each study costs less, the same budget can cover more questions, more regions or repeated surveys over several years.

**Probability and Non-Probability Sampling**

The major difference lies in how the units are picked and whether every unit has a known chance of being picked.

In probability sampling, each member of the population has a known, non-zero chance of selection, and the selection is made by a random process, not by the researcher's choice. A sampling frame, meaning a complete list of the population, is normally needed. The common types are simple random sampling (every member has an equal chance, as in a lottery draw), systematic sampling (every kth member from a list), stratified sampling (the population is split into groups such as age bands, and a random sample is drawn from each) and cluster sampling (whole groups such as villages or classrooms are picked at random). Because chance decides, personal bias is kept out, and the researcher can calculate sampling error and generalise the findings to the population with stated confidence. The weakness is that it takes more effort, a good frame and more money.

In non-probability sampling, selection depends on the researcher's judgement or on convenience, and the chance of any particular member being chosen is unknown. Examples include convenience sampling (asking whoever is easily available), judgement or purposive sampling (choosing people believed to be most informative), quota sampling (filling set numbers from each category without random selection) and snowball sampling (existing participants lead to new ones). These methods are quick, cheap and useful when no list exists, for instance when studying a hidden or rare group, or in a pilot study. However, there is a risk of bias, sampling error cannot be measured properly, and the results cannot safely be stretched to the whole population.

To see the difference in a simple case, imagine a college with 4,000 students. If the researcher draws 200 roll numbers by lottery, every student had the same chance and the result can be applied to the whole college. If the researcher instead stands at the canteen and asks the first 200 students who walk by, the group may be full of people who skip lectures, and nobody can say how far the answers differ from those of the college as a whole.

Put simply, probability sampling relies on random selection and allows statements about the population backed by numbers; non-probability sampling relies on human choice and gives findings that describe only the people actually studied.

**Conclusion**

Sampling is attractive because it makes research affordable, quick and manageable, allows closer supervision, and is the only option when testing destroys the item or the population cannot be fully reached. It also lets the researcher measure how reliable the result is, provided the sample is chosen well. The choice between the two broad approaches depends on the purpose. If the aim is to draw conclusions about the entire population with a known margin of error, probability sampling is the right choice. If the aim is exploration, speed or reaching a hard-to-find group, non-probability sampling can serve, as long as its limits are acknowledged.
