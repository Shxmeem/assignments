**Short Notes on Non-Probability Sampling Methods, the Central Limit Theorem and the Correlation Coefficient**

**Introduction**

Statistics gives researchers tools to collect data, to understand how sample results behave, and to describe how variables move together. The three topics below each belong to one of these areas. Non-probability sampling concerns how a sample is picked, the Central Limit Theorem explains why sample averages can be trusted, and the correlation coefficient measures the link between two variables.

**1. Non-Probability Sampling Methods**

In non-probability sampling, the researcher does not use a random process to choose the units. Selection depends on judgement, convenience or the willingness of people to take part, so the chance of any member being chosen is not known. The methods are as follows.

- Convenience sampling: the researcher picks whoever is easy to reach, such as shoppers outside a store or classmates in a hall.
- Judgement (purposive) sampling: the researcher deliberately selects people who are believed to know the subject best, such as senior doctors for a study on hospital procedures.
- Quota sampling: the population is divided into categories like gender or age group, and a fixed number is filled from each category, but the people inside each quota are chosen by the interviewer, not by chance.
- Snowball sampling: a few initial participants are found, and they introduce others from the same group. This suits hard-to-find groups such as migrant workers or people with a rare condition.

These methods save time and money and work even when no list of the population exists. Their main drawback is bias. Sampling error cannot be calculated, so the findings should not be applied to the whole population with any claim of precision.

**2. Central Limit Theorem**

The Central Limit Theorem states that if we take many random samples of the same size from a population and calculate the mean of each, the distribution of those sample means will be approximately normal. This holds when the sample size is large enough, whatever the shape of the original population. As a common rule, a size of 30 or more is usually adequate, though a heavily skewed population may need more.

Two further points go with it. The average of the sample means equals the population mean. The spread of the sample means, called the standard error, equals the population standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size, so larger samples give tighter results.

Its importance is practical. Even when the income of a city's households is lopsided, with a few very rich families, the mean income of repeated samples will still follow a normal pattern. This allows researchers to build confidence intervals and carry out tests of hypotheses using normal probabilities.

**3. The Correlation Coefficient**

The correlation coefficient, usually written r, is a single number that shows the direction and strength of a straight-line relationship between two variables. Its value always lies between -1 and +1.

- A value near +1 means both variables rise together, as with hours of study and marks.
- A value near -1 means one rises while the other falls, as with the price of a product and the quantity bought.
- A value near 0 means there is little or no straight-line relationship.

The most common form is Karl Pearson's coefficient, which is the covariance of the two variables divided by the product of their standard deviations. When the data are ranks or the relationship is not linear, Spearman's rank correlation is used instead.

Two cautions are needed. First, r only captures straight-line links, so a strong curved relationship can still give a value close to zero. Second, correlation does not prove cause. Ice cream sales and drowning cases both rise in summer, yet one does not cause the other.

**Conclusion**

The three notes fit together in practice. A researcher first decides how to select the sample, and non-probability methods are useful when speed or access matters more than precision. When random samples are available, the Central Limit Theorem supports drawing conclusions from them. Once the data are in hand, the correlation coefficient helps describe how two measured quantities are related. Knowing the limits of each tool is as important as knowing how to use it.
