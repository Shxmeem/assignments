# Communication Process and the Value of Feedback in Organisations

## 1. The communication process

Communication is the passing of information from one person to another so that what the listener understands matches what the speaker meant. People often treat it as finished once the message is sent, but it is only finished when the message has been understood.

The process has a sender with something to say, the encoding of that idea into words, numbers or tone, a channel that carries it, a receiver who decodes it, and feedback that travels back to the sender. Noise can interfere at any point.

```
Sender ──► Encoding ──► Channel ──► Decoding ──► Receiver
  ▲        (message)       ▲                         │
  │                      NOISE                       │
  └───────────────── FEEDBACK ◄──────────────────────┘
```

A plant manager wants a new safety check to start on Monday. She writes a notice (encoding) and pins it on the main board (channel). The day shift reads it, but the night shift never passes the board, so distance and timing act as noise. A night supervisor hears about it second-hand and asks, "Does this apply to us too?" That question is feedback, and it is the only way the manager learns that her message missed half the people it was meant for.

## 2. Why feedback matters for organisations now

Without feedback, the sender is only guessing. Three things make that guess riskier than it used to be.

First, many teams are spread across cities and time zones. In an office, a puzzled face tells you to explain again. In a chat thread, silence could mean agreement, confusion, or that nobody read it. Feedback now has to be asked for on purpose.

Second, customers no longer wait to be asked. A late delivery or a confusing policy turns into a public post within minutes. An organisation with its own channels for collecting and answering complaints hears about problems earlier than one that finds out from social media.

Third, employees expect to know how they are doing during the year, not at an annual appraisal that arrives months after the mistake or the good work. A wrong instruction corrected on day one costs a short conversation, but corrected after a quarter it costs rework.

## 3. Organisations that show it

**Toyota.** On its assembly lines, any worker who spots a defect can pull the andon cord. This signals for help and, if the problem is not fixed in time, stops the line. The person closest to the work is treated as the one who knows, and the defect is dealt with on the spot rather than travelling down the line.

**Adobe.** In 2012 Adobe dropped its yearly performance rating and moved to regular "Check-in" conversations between managers and employees. Feedback became an ongoing exchange instead of a once-a-year verdict, and the company reported fewer resignations afterwards.

**Netflix.** Netflix expects people to give direct feedback to colleagues, including senior ones, and leaders are expected to ask for it about themselves. Reed Hastings and Erin Meyer describe this practice in their book *No Rules Rules*.

**Nokia (what happens without it).** Researchers Timo Vuori and Quy Huy studied the company's loss of the smartphone market and found that middle managers feared the reaction of senior leaders. They softened bad news about competitors and about Nokia's own software, so the top team worked from a more comfortable picture than the real one, and decisions came late.

## 4. Conclusion

Feedback turns communication from sending into a two-way exchange. It works when it is specific, comes soon after the event, and people can see something change because of it. Toyota and Adobe built it into routine work, while Nokia shows what it costs when bad news stops moving upward.
