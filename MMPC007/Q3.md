# Non-Verbal Communication in Professional Settings

## Introduction

Non-verbal communication is everything people convey without words: posture, facial expression, gestures, eye contact, tone of voice and silence. At work it runs alongside speech, and when the two disagree, listeners usually believe the non-verbal part. Albert Mehrabian's studies in the 1960s found this for messages about feelings and attitudes. His figures are often quoted as if they covered all communication, which they do not, but the basic point holds. A manager who says "I'm happy to help" while checking her phone has not said it at all.

## Body language

Body language is the overall picture that posture, movement, gestures and expression make together. It shows how a person feels about what is being said. A candidate who answers every question well but sits leaning back with folded arms may seem defensive. One signal alone can mislead, since crossed arms may only mean the room is cold. It is better to look for several signals together, or a change from the person's usual manner, and then ask.

## Facial expressions

The face shows interest, doubt and irritation faster than words can. A negotiator who sees doubt flicker across the other side's face when a price is named can stop and ask, "Does that seem high?" instead of pressing on. Faces also give the speaker away. A forced smile while announcing a pay freeze looks insincere and costs more trust than a plain, serious expression would.

## Gestures

Hands add emphasis and help explain size, shape and sequence. A trainer who counts steps on her fingers is easier to follow. Gestures also differ across cultures. A thumbs-up is friendly in many places and rude in a few, and a head nod may mean "I agree" or only "I am listening". In a mixed team it is safer to keep gestures plain and check understanding in words.

## Posture

Posture signals attention and status. Sitting upright and slightly forward tells a speaker you are following, while slouching or turning towards the door says the opposite. A manager who stands over a seated employee makes disagreement harder. Sitting at the same level, or at an angle instead of across a desk, lowers tension.

## Eye contact

In most Western workplaces steady eye contact signals attention and honesty, and avoiding it can seem evasive. In some cultures, looking directly at a senior person is disrespectful, so a junior employee looking down may be showing courtesy, not doubt. On video calls people tend to watch the faces on screen rather than the camera, so the other side feels ignored. Looking at the camera when making a key point fixes this.

## Significance for managerial effectiveness

Managers spend most of the day in conversation, and staff watch them closely because they control tasks, pay and promotion. Non-verbal behaviour affects their work in three ways.

**Trust.** Employees notice whether a manager's face and tone match her words. When they match, people believe her. When they do not, people trust the signals.

**Listening.** A manager who nods, keeps eye contact and puts her phone away gets fuller answers. Staff tell her about problems early because she looks like she wants to hear them.

**Reading the team.** A manager who notices that a talkative member has gone quiet, or that the room stiffened after an announcement, can raise it before it turns into a complaint or a resignation.

Non-verbal signals also change how instructions land. "Please redo this report" sounds like a request in a calm voice, and like a reprimand with a sigh and folded arms.

## Conclusion

Words carry the content of a message, while posture, expression, gestures and eye contact show how it is meant and whether it is believed. A manager who controls her own signals and reads those of others loses fewer messages and builds more trust. She should still treat what she sees as a clue to ask about, not a verdict.
