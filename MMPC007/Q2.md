# Barriers to Communication in the Workplace

## Introduction

A barrier is anything that changes or blocks a message between the person who sends it and the person who receives it. In real workplaces they seldom come one at a time. A manager gives an instruction in a noisy workshop, to an employee who is nervous around her, using a term the employee has never heard. Three barriers sit on one sentence. They are usually studied in four groups: physical, psychological, semantic and organisational.

## 1. Physical barriers

These come from the surroundings and the equipment used to communicate: machine noise, closed cabins, distance between floors, a poor phone line, a projector that fails, an inbox with hundreds of unread mails.

The impact is mostly on accuracy and speed. A supervisor who shouts a changed instruction across a shop floor may be half heard, and the worker acts on the half. In remote teams a weak connection chops sentences into pieces, and people often stop asking for repeats so they do not hold up the meeting. These are the easiest barriers to fix. A written confirmation, a quieter room or better equipment usually solves them.

## 2. Psychological barriers

These sit in the minds of the sender or receiver: fear, stress, prejudice, fixed assumptions, distrust and plain inattention.

Their impact is harder to spot because nothing looks wrong from outside. A new joiner who fears looking ignorant nods along without understanding. A manager who has already decided a colleague's ideas are weak stops listening halfway. An employee worried about job cuts reads a routine circular as a warning. The results are silence, hidden mistakes, and rumours that fill the gaps where information should have been.

## 3. Semantic barriers

These come from language itself: words with more than one meaning, technical jargon, accents and dialects, careless translation, vague phrasing, and tone that gets lost in writing.

"Send it by end of day" leaves open whose day is meant, especially across time zones. "Urgent" may mean within the hour to one person and within the week to another. In a software company a developer may call a task "done" when the code is written, while the tester takes it to mean checked. The usual cost is rework and arguments about who said what.

At its worst the cost is far higher. In the 1977 Tenerife airport collision, in which 583 people died, unclear radio phrasing and interference on the frequency contributed to one crew starting its take-off run while another aircraft was still on the runway.

## 4. Organisational barriers

These come from structure, rules and culture: too many levels of management, rigid chains of command, unclear roles, departments that keep information to themselves, heavy paperwork, and no proper route for bad news to travel upward.

When a message passes through many hands, each person trims or rewords it, and the last listener hears something different from what the first one said. Departments working in isolation create gaps, such as a sales team promising a feature the engineers were never told about.

The most serious effect is on upward communication. Before the 1986 Challenger launch, engineers at Morton Thiokol had warned about the rubber seals in cold weather. The warning did not stop the launch, and the commission that investigated afterwards criticised how it was handled and passed on.

## How they add up, and what helps

Together these barriers cost an organisation errors, repeated work, missed deadlines and lower trust between people. A few practical steps deal with more than one barrier at once:

- Confirm important instructions in writing.
- Use plain words and explain any technical term.
- Ask the listener to say back the key point.
- Make it normal for someone to say, "I did not follow that."
- Give junior staff a clear route to report a problem, and let them see that someone acts on it.

## Conclusion

Physical barriers decide whether a message arrives, psychological ones whether it is heard honestly, semantic ones whether it is understood, and organisational ones whether it travels the right way. The first and third are mostly solved by care and clarity. The second and fourth need changes in how people are treated and how the organisation is run, which is why they last longer.
