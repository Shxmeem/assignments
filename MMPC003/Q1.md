Introduction

National income is the total value of goods and services produced by a country in a year, counted in money terms. It shows how much the people of a country have earned and produced over that period. Measuring it is not easy, since millions of people and businesses are involved, but every country does it. This assignment explains why national income is estimated and discusses the three main approaches used to estimate it.

Need for Estimation of National Income

1. To know the size of the economy. National income tells us how big the economy is. Without a figure, we cannot say whether a country is rich or poor, or how it compares with others.

2. To measure growth. When national income is compared across years, we can see whether the economy is growing, standing still or shrinking. Growth in the figure, after removing the effect of price rise, shows whether production is actually increasing.

3. To judge the standard of living. Dividing national income by the population gives income per person. This is a rough guide to how well people live. It is also used to compare one country with another, although it does not show how evenly the income is shared.

4. To plan and make policy. The government needs these figures to frame its budget, fix tax rates, decide how much to spend and plan its development. For example, if the figures show weak growth, the government may spend more on roads and schools to create jobs.

5. To understand the structure of the economy. National income is broken down into farming, industry and services. This shows which sector is growing and which is lagging, so that attention can go to the weak area.

6. To study how income is distributed. The figures show how much goes to wages, rent, interest and profit. This helps in studying whether workers or owners are getting a larger share, and whether the gap between rich and poor is widening.

7. To measure savings and investment. National income data shows how much of the income is saved and invested. This tells us whether the country is putting enough aside for future growth.

8. To compare across countries and over time. Institutions such as international bodies use national income to compare countries, decide on loans and set contributions. Businesses also use it to judge demand in a market.

Approaches to Estimating National Income

There are three ways of measuring national income. They look at the same flow of money from three sides: what is produced, who earns it and how it is spent. In theory all three should give the same answer.

1. Product Method (Value Added Method)

This method adds up the value of all final goods and services produced in the country in a year. Only final goods are counted, meaning goods that go to the end user. If we counted flour, then bread made from it, we would count the flour twice. To avoid this, either only the final product is counted, or only the value added at each stage is counted.

For example, a farmer sells wheat to a mill for 100. The mill sells flour to a bakery for 150, and the bakery sells bread for 220. The value added is 100 by the farmer, 50 by the mill and 70 by the bakery. The total is 220, which is the price of the bread.

The economy is divided into sectors such as farming, mining, manufacturing and services, and the value added by each is totalled. Some adjustments are needed. Goods produced and used by the producer, such as a farmer's own food, are included by estimating their value, and so is the rent of a house that its owner lives in. Second-hand goods and shares are left out, as they are not new production.

This method is useful because it shows the contribution of each sector. Its weakness is that much production, especially in small farms and informal work, is not recorded, so values have to be estimated.

2. Income Method

This method adds up the incomes earned by all the people who helped in production. These include wages and salaries of workers, rent for land and buildings, interest on money lent, and profit of the owners. The income of people who work for themselves, such as small shopkeepers and farmers, is also counted.

Only earnings from production are included. Pensions, gifts, scholarships and lottery winnings are not counted, because they are transfers and no new production took place. Profits from selling old assets are also left out.

This method is useful for studying how income is shared among workers, landlords, lenders and owners. Its main difficulty is that people may not report their true income, and many self-employed people do not keep records, so the figure may be too low.

3. Expenditure Method

This method adds up the total spending on final goods and services in a year. It has four parts: spending by households on consumption, spending by businesses on investment such as machinery and buildings, spending by the government on goods and services, and net exports, which is exports minus imports. Imports are subtracted because they are spending on goods made abroad.

Spending on intermediate goods is not counted, to avoid counting the same item twice. Transfer payments like pensions are not counted either, because they are not payment for goods or services.

This method is helpful for knowing what drives the economy, whether it is consumer demand, investment or government spending. Its weakness is that data on household spending and business investment is hard to collect fully, and errors in estimating imports and stock changes can affect the result.

Conclusion

National income is estimated to know the size and growth of the economy, to judge living standards, to plan policy and to see how income is shared. It is measured by three methods, product, income and expenditure, which look at production, earnings and spending. In practice, countries use a mix of them depending on the data available, since each has its own gaps.
