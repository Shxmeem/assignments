# National Income: Why We Estimate It and How It Is Measured

## Introduction

National income is the total value of the goods and services produced by a country's people in one year, usually counted in money terms. It is like the annual income statement of a country. Just as a family or business needs to know how much it earns, a nation needs to know the size of its economy and how it is changing.

## Need for Estimating National Income

**1. To know the size of the economy.** National income shows how much the country produces in a year. Without it, there is no way to judge whether the economy is large or small, or whether it is growing or shrinking.

**2. To measure economic growth.** Comparing national income across years shows how fast the economy is growing. If it rises faster than the population, people are on average becoming better off. If it falls, the country is in trouble.

**3. To find the standard of living.** Dividing national income by population gives per capita income. It is a rough guide to how well people live on average and is used to compare one country with another.

**4. To plan and make policy.** The government uses national income figures to set targets in five-year or annual plans, decide how much to spend and tax, and judge whether past policies worked. Without these figures, planning is guesswork.

**5. To understand the structure of the economy.** The estimates show how much comes from agriculture, industry and services, and how much is saved, invested and spent. This helps in seeing which sectors are growing and which need support. In India, for example, the large rise in the share of services is visible through these figures.

**6. To study the distribution of income.** Income-based estimates show how much goes to workers as wages, to owners of land as rent, to lenders as interest and to businesses as profit. This helps in studying inequality.

**7. For international comparison and decisions.** Bodies such as the World Bank and the IMF use national income to compare countries, decide on loans and fix contributions to international organisations.

**8. For business decisions.** Companies use the figures to judge the size of the market, and to decide where to invest and how much to produce.

## Approaches to Estimating National Income

There are three main methods. They look at the same flow of money from three sides: what is produced, who earns it and how it is spent. In theory all three give the same answer, because every rupee produced becomes someone's income, and every rupee of income is either spent or saved.

### 1. Product Method (Value Added Method)

This method adds up the value of all final goods and services produced in the country in a year.

To avoid counting the same thing twice, only **final goods** are counted, such as bread sold to a consumer, and not intermediate goods, such as the wheat and flour used to make it. Alternatively, we add the **value added** at each stage, which is the sales value minus the cost of materials bought from others.

*Example:* A farmer sells wheat for ₹100 to a miller. The miller sells flour for ₹150 to a baker. The baker sells bread for ₹200. The value added is ₹100 + ₹50 + ₹50 = ₹200, which equals the value of the final bread. Counting all three sales (₹450) would be double counting.

**Steps:**
- Divide the economy into sectors (agriculture, industry, services).
- Find the value added in each sector.
- Add them to get Gross Domestic Product at factor cost or market price.
- Subtract depreciation and adjust for taxes, subsidies and income from abroad to get national income.

**Best suited for:** sectors where production can be measured, such as agriculture and manufacturing.

**Difficulties:** Goods produced and consumed at home are hard to count, such as vegetables from one's own garden. Many small producers keep no records. Secondhand goods must be left out, since they were counted when first produced.

### 2. Income Method

This method adds up all the incomes earned by people who took part in production in a year.

The factors of production earn:
- Wages and salaries (labour)
- Rent (land)
- Interest (capital)
- Profit (enterprise)
- Mixed income of self-employed people, such as small shopkeepers and farmers

**National Income = Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit + Mixed income + Net income from abroad**

Only earnings from productive work are counted. **Transfer payments**, such as pensions, scholarships and unemployment allowances, are left out because they are not payment for current production. Gifts, lottery winnings and income from selling old assets, such as a secondhand car, are also excluded.

**Best suited for:** sectors where incomes are recorded, such as organised industry and government services.

**Difficulties:** Many people, particularly in the informal sector, do not report their incomes. Tax evasion and black money lead to under-reporting. It is hard to separate the income of self-employed people into wages, profit and rent.

### 3. Expenditure Method

This method adds up all the money spent on final goods and services in a year.

**GDP at market price = C + I + G + (X − M)**

Where:
- **C** = private consumption spending by households
- **I** = investment, including business spending on machinery, buildings and stock
- **G** = government spending on goods and services
- **X − M** = exports minus imports (net exports)

Only spending on final goods is counted. Spending on intermediate goods is left out, and imports are subtracted because they were produced abroad.

**Best suited for:** the economy as a whole, as it matches closely with how national accounts are put together.

**Difficulties:** It is hard to collect accurate data on household spending, especially in rural and informal areas. It is difficult to separate consumption from investment in some cases, for example spending on education or a house. Estimating changes in stock held by businesses is also hard.

### Which method is used in practice?

No single method works for all sectors, so most countries, including India, use a combination. In India, the National Statistical Office under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation estimates national income. The production method is used for agriculture, mining, and manufacturing, and the income method for sectors such as some services, with expenditure estimates prepared separately to cross-check. This is because each method is more reliable for some sectors than for others.

## Related Terms

Several measures come from these methods:

- **GDP (Gross Domestic Product):** value of all final goods and services produced within the country's borders, including output by foreigners.
- **GNP (Gross National Product):** GDP plus net income earned from abroad by the country's residents.
- **NNP (Net National Product):** GNP minus depreciation, which is the wear and tear of capital.
- **National Income:** NNP at factor cost, which is NNP at market price minus indirect taxes plus subsidies.

## Limitations of National Income Estimates

- Activities that do not pass through markets, such as housework and unpaid care, are not counted, although they are valuable.
- The informal economy and black money are partly missed, which is a large problem in India.
- National income says nothing about how fairly income is shared.
- It does not count the damage to the environment from production, such as pollution and loss of forests.
- Changes in prices can make comparison across years misleading, so figures must be adjusted for inflation by using constant prices.
- It measures production, not happiness or wellbeing.

## Conclusion

National income estimates are needed to know the size and growth of the economy, to compare living standards, to plan government policy and to understand how the economy is structured. The three methods, product, income and expenditure, measure the same total from different sides, and each has strengths and weaknesses. In practice, a mix is used, depending on the data available for each sector. Although the figures are imperfect and leave out unpaid work, the informal sector and environmental costs, they remain the main tool for understanding and managing an economy.
