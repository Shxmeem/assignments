# The World Bank Group and the Difference Between the IMF and the World Bank

## Introduction

The World Bank and the International Monetary Fund were both created at the Bretton Woods Conference in 1944, as the world was preparing to rebuild after the Second World War. People often mix them up because both are based in Washington, D.C., both are owned by member countries and both lend money. But they were set up for different jobs. This assignment lists the institutions that make up the World Bank Group and then explains how the IMF differs from the World Bank.

## The World Bank Group Institutions

The World Bank Group is not one organisation. It is a family of five institutions, each with its own role. The name "World Bank" usually refers only to the first two.

**1. International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD), 1944.** This is the original World Bank. It was first set up to help rebuild Europe after the war and now lends to middle-income and creditworthy low-income countries. It raises most of its money by selling bonds in the world's financial markets and lends at interest rates lower than these countries could get on their own.

**2. International Development Association (IDA), 1960.** IDA helps the poorest countries. It gives loans at very low or zero interest with long repayment periods, and also gives grants. Its money comes mainly from contributions by richer member countries, which are topped up every three years.

**3. International Finance Corporation (IFC), 1956.** IFC works with the private sector in developing countries. It lends to private companies, buys shares in them and gives advice, so that businesses can grow and create jobs. Unlike the first two, it deals directly with companies, not governments.

**4. Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA), 1988.** MIGA gives insurance to foreign investors against non-commercial risks such as war, civil disturbance, a government breaking a contract or stopping the transfer of money abroad. This encourages investors to put money into riskier countries.

**5. International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID), 1966.** ICSID does not lend or invest. It offers a neutral place for conciliation and arbitration when a foreign investor and a host government have a dispute. This reduces investors' fear that they will not get a fair hearing.

IBRD and IDA together are usually called the World Bank. The stated goals of the whole group are to end extreme poverty and to share prosperity more widely.

## About the IMF

The International Monetary Fund has a different purpose. Its main job is to keep the international monetary system stable. This means helping countries keep their currencies and payments in order, so that trade and investment can continue smoothly. It does this through three main activities:

- **Surveillance:** it monitors the economies of its members and the world economy, and gives policy advice, mainly through regular reviews of each country.
- **Lending:** it gives short and medium-term loans to countries facing a balance of payments problem, which means they cannot pay for imports or repay foreign debts. These loans normally come with conditions that the country must meet, such as cutting its deficit or changing certain policies.
- **Technical help and training:** it advises governments and central banks on matters such as taxation, banking supervision and statistics.

The IMF also issues a reserve asset called Special Drawing Rights (SDRs), which members can exchange for usable currencies.

## Difference Between the IMF and the World Bank

| Basis | IMF | World Bank |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Keep the global monetary and financial system stable | Reduce poverty and support long-term development |
| Focus | Short-term problems, such as a shortage of foreign exchange or a financial crisis | Long-term needs, such as roads, schools, health and energy |
| Type of lending | Gives loans to help countries through a payments crisis; money is used to support the country's budget or reserves | Gives loans and grants for specific projects and reforms in sectors |
| Who it lends to | Any member country that has a payments problem, rich or poor | Mainly developing countries (private companies through IFC) |
| Source of funds | Quotas paid by member countries, with some borrowing | Member contributions, plus bonds sold in financial markets (IBRD) and donor funds (IDA) |
| Conditions | Policy conditions related to the economy, such as deficit control and exchange rate reform | Conditions linked to the project or reform being funded |
| Structure | A single institution | A group of five institutions |
| Voting | Votes depend on a member's quota, which reflects the size of its economy | Votes depend on a member's shareholding, which is also weighted by economic strength |
| Staff | Mostly economists and financial experts | A wider range, including engineers, health and education specialists and agricultural experts |
| Role in a crisis | Acts as a lender to stabilise a country's finances | Helps with rebuilding and development afterwards |

A simple way to remember the difference: the IMF is like an emergency doctor who treats a sudden financial problem and stabilises the patient, while the World Bank is like a long-term health and education planner who helps the patient become stronger over the years.

## Overlaps and Criticisms

The two institutions work closely and often at the same time in a country. When a country gets an IMF loan, the World Bank may support reforms in areas such as social spending or banking. Their membership also overlaps, since a country must be a member of the IMF before it can join the World Bank. India is a founding member of both.

Both have been criticised. The conditions attached to IMF loans have often been blamed for hurting poor people, because cuts in public spending can reduce health, education and subsidies. The structural adjustment programmes of the 1980s and 1990s are the usual example. Developing countries also complain that the richer countries, especially the United States, hold a larger share of the votes and have more influence, though the share of countries like India and China has been raised gradually. The World Bank has been criticised for projects that displaced people or damaged the environment and for imposing policies that did not suit local conditions. Both institutions have since changed their approach to some extent, with more attention to social protection, to the environment and to what countries themselves want.

## Conclusion

The World Bank Group consists of five institutions: IBRD, IDA, IFC, MIGA and ICSID. Together they lend to governments, support private business, insure investments and settle investment disputes, all with the goal of promoting development. The IMF is a separate institution whose job is to keep the global monetary system stable and help countries in temporary payments trouble. The simplest difference is that the IMF deals with short-term financial stability and the World Bank with long-term development. They are separate but complementary, and a country often needs both.
