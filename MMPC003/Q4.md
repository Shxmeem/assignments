# Question

Identify the World Bank Group Institutions. Differentiate between IMF and the World Bank.

# Introduction

After the Second World War, many countries were damaged and the world needed a way to rebuild and keep its money systems steady. In 1944, at a conference held at Bretton Woods in the United States, the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank were set up for this purpose. They are often mentioned together, and people confuse their work. This assignment identifies the institutions that make up the World Bank Group and then explains how the IMF and the World Bank differ.

# Institutions of the World Bank Group

The World Bank Group has **five institutions**. All work towards reducing poverty and helping countries develop, but each has its own task.

1. **International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD).** It was the first institution, set up in 1944 to help rebuild countries after the war. Today it lends to middle-income countries and to some poorer countries that are creditworthy. It raises most of its money by selling bonds in the world's financial markets and lends at interest, for projects such as roads, power plants, schools and hospitals. Borrowing countries repay with interest, which keeps the bank running.

2. **International Development Association (IDA).** It was set up in 1960 to help the poorest countries. It gives loans on very easy terms, with little or no interest and a long time to repay, and also gives grants. Its money comes mainly from contributions by richer member countries. The IBRD and IDA together are what people usually call the World Bank.

3. **International Finance Corporation (IFC).** It was set up in 1956 and works with the private sector in developing countries. It gives loans, buys shares in companies and advises businesses. Its aim is to create jobs and growth by supporting private firms that banks may hesitate to fund. Its loans do not need a government guarantee.

4. **Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA).** It was set up in 1988. It gives insurance to investors against non-commercial risks in developing countries, such as war, seizure of property by a government or the government breaking a contract. This encourages foreign companies to invest in risky countries. Its cover can also protect banks that lend to such projects.

5. **International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID).** It was set up in 1966. It provides a place for settling disputes between foreign investors and the governments of the countries they invest in, through conciliation and arbitration. This gives both sides a neutral place to resolve a disagreement.

# Difference Between the IMF and the World Bank

Both have their headquarters in Washington, D.C., both have nearly all countries of the world as members and both were set up at Bretton Woods. But they have different purposes and different ways of working.

1. **Main purpose.** The IMF is concerned with the stability of the world's money system. It watches over exchange rates, trade payments and the money position of countries. The World Bank is concerned with development, that is, reducing poverty and building the economy of a country over the long term.

2. **Type of problem handled.** The IMF deals with short-term problems, such as a country running short of foreign currency to pay for imports or to repay debts, which is called a balance of payments problem. The World Bank deals with long-term needs such as building infrastructure, schools, health services and farm projects.

3. **Type of lending.** The IMF lends money to a government to help it get through a financial crisis, and the money is used to support the country's finances in general. The World Bank lends for specific projects and programmes, and also for reforms in sectors such as education or health.

4. **Conditions.** IMF loans usually come with conditions on the country's economic policies, such as controlling the budget deficit, bringing down inflation or changing exchange rate policy. The World Bank also attaches conditions, but they are more tied to the project or programme being funded.

5. **Source of funds.** The IMF gets its money mainly from the quotas that each member country pays, based on the size of its economy. The World Bank raises most of its funds by selling bonds in the financial markets, and also gets contributions from richer countries, particularly for IDA.

6. **Who it serves.** The IMF can lend to any member country, rich or poor, that has a financial problem. The World Bank focuses on developing countries.

7. **Structure and staff.** The IMF is a single organisation led by a Managing Director. The World Bank is a group of five institutions, led by a President. IMF staff are mostly economists and financial experts, while the World Bank employs a wider group, including engineers, health and education specialists and farm experts.

8. **Voting.** In both, voting power depends on how much a country contributes, so richer countries have more say. The IMF votes follow its quotas, and the World Bank votes follow the shares held by each country.

9. **Other work.** Apart from lending, the IMF reviews the economies of its members every year and gives advice and training on managing money matters. The World Bank also gives advice and publishes research and data on development.

10. **Example.** If a country faces a sudden fall in its foreign currency and cannot pay for essential imports, it may go to the IMF. If a country wants to build a highway network or improve rural water supply, it may go to the World Bank.

# Conclusion

The World Bank Group includes the IBRD, IDA, IFC, MIGA and ICSID, each with its own role in helping countries develop and attract investment. The IMF and the World Bank came out of the same conference and often work together, but the IMF looks after short-term financial stability, while the World Bank supports long-term development through projects and reforms.
