# The New Economic Policy of 1991 and Atmanirbhar Bharat

## Introduction

In 1991, India changed the way it ran its economy. For four decades before that, the government controlled industry, trade and prices closely. The New Economic Policy replaced much of that control with competition and openness. Nearly thirty years later, in 2020, the government announced Atmanirbhar Bharat (self-reliant India) in response to a different kind of crisis. This assignment describes the 1991 policy and then examines whether Atmanirbhar Bharat can fairly be called an improvement over it.

## Background: Why the Policy Was Needed

By 1991, India faced a serious balance of payments crisis. Foreign exchange reserves had fallen so low that they could pay for only about two to three weeks of imports. Several things had led to this:

- The government was spending far more than it earned, and the fiscal deficit was large.
- Inflation was high.
- The Gulf War of 1990–91 pushed up oil prices and reduced money sent home by Indians working in the Gulf.
- The public sector was often inefficient, and licensing and import controls had made Indian industry costly and slow to improve.
- The country had to pledge gold to raise emergency funds and sought a loan from the International Monetary Fund, which came with conditions for reform.

The government under Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao, with Dr. Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister, used this crisis to bring in wide reforms.

## The New Economic Policy (1991)

The reforms are usually grouped under three headings: liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation.

**1. Liberalisation (reducing government control).**

- *Industrial licensing was abolished* for most industries. Earlier, a firm needed government permission to start, expand or change its products. Only a short list, such as defence equipment, hazardous chemicals and a few others, stayed under licensing.
- *Limits on large firms were removed.* The restrictions on the size of big companies under the MRTP Act were relaxed, so they could grow and compete.
- *Financial sector reforms* followed. Interest rates were gradually freed, the cash and liquidity requirements for banks were reduced, and private banks were allowed to open.
- *Tax reform.* Very high tax rates were lowered and the system simplified.

**2. Privatisation (reducing the role of the state).**

- The list of industries reserved only for the public sector was cut sharply, from 17 to a handful.
- The government began selling part of its shares in public companies (disinvestment) to raise money and improve efficiency.
- Loss-making public enterprises were no longer assured of unlimited support.

**3. Globalisation (opening to the world).**

- *The rupee was devalued* in July 1991 to make exports cheaper and imports costlier. It was later allowed to be determined mostly by the market.
- *Import licences were removed* for most goods, and import duties were reduced steeply.
- *Foreign investment was welcomed.* Automatic approval was given for foreign investment up to 51 per cent in many priority industries, and foreign institutional investors were allowed into the stock market.
- *Foreign exchange rules were eased* over time, leading to the replacement of the strict FERA with the more liberal FEMA.

### Results of the 1991 Reforms

- The economy grew faster, and growth rose to around 6 to 7 per cent a year in later decades.
- Foreign exchange reserves rose a great deal, and the crisis of 1991 did not return.
- Services, especially information technology, grew rapidly and made India a global player.
- Consumers gained more choice and better quality, in goods ranging from cars to phones.
- Poverty fell substantially as incomes rose.

### Criticisms of the 1991 Reforms

- Growth created fewer jobs than expected, especially in factories. Much of the new employment was in low-paid informal work.
- Agriculture was neglected and grew slowly, so rural distress continued.
- Inequality between rich and poor and between states grew.
- Manufacturing did not grow as quickly as services, and its share in the economy remained stagnant at around 15 to 17 per cent.
- India became heavily dependent on imports for important goods such as electronics, machinery and pharmaceutical ingredients, with China as a major supplier.

## Atmanirbhar Bharat (2020)

Atmanirbhar Bharat was announced in May 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic, together with a relief and reform package of about ₹20 lakh crore, often described as around 10 per cent of GDP, though the actual cost to the government was lower because much of it consisted of loan guarantees and credit support. The pandemic exposed how much India depended on imports for essentials such as protective equipment, medical devices and key components, and how fragile global supply chains could be.

It is important to note that the policy does not mean isolation. The government has described it as becoming strong enough to compete globally, not as shutting out the world. Its main elements are:

- **Five pillars:** economy, infrastructure, systems, demography and demand.
- **Support for small businesses (MSMEs).** The definition of a small business was revised so firms could grow without losing benefits, and collateral-free loans with a government guarantee were provided to help firms survive the lockdown.
- **Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes.** Manufacturers in selected sectors, such as mobile phones, electronics, pharmaceuticals, automobiles and solar equipment, receive payments based on the additional goods they produce in India.
- **Strategic sectors.** The limit for automatic foreign investment in defence was raised, lists of defence items that will not be imported were announced, coal mining was opened to private companies and mission-style programmes were begun for sectors such as semiconductors.
- **Agriculture.** A fund for farm infrastructure such as storage and cold chains was set up, and reforms to farm marketing were attempted.
- **Labour and business reforms.** Labour laws were consolidated into four codes, and the government spoke of simpler rules for business.
- **Vocal for local.** Citizens and businesses were encouraged to buy and promote Indian products.

## Is Atmanirbhar Bharat an Improvement Over 1991?

The answer is partly yes, but it is more accurate to call it a correction and extension than a replacement.

### Where it can be called an improvement

| Area | 1991 policy | Atmanirbhar Bharat |
|---|---|---|
| Main aim | Escape a crisis, open the economy and raise growth | Build strong domestic production and reduce risky dependence |
| Focus sector | Services and trade grew most; manufacturing lagged | Manufacturing is the main target, through PLI and other incentives |
| Role of government | Step back and let markets decide | Step in selectively to support chosen sectors and fix gaps |
| Imports | Made freely available | Reduce dependence on imports for critical goods |
| Small firms | Had little direct support | MSMEs get credit support and a more favourable definition |
| Supply chains | Not a major concern | Resilience seen as a priority after COVID |
| Strategic goods | Not specifically addressed | Defence, semiconductors and energy treated as priorities |

**1. It addresses what 1991 left unfinished.** The 1991 reforms opened the economy but did not build a strong manufacturing base, which is what creates large numbers of jobs. Atmanirbhar Bharat targets this directly.

**2. It deals with import dependence.** After 1991, imports of key items grew. A country that depends heavily on a single supplier for important goods is exposed to shocks, as the pandemic showed. The policy tries to reduce this risk.

**3. It supports small businesses.** MSMEs employ many people, but the 1991 reforms mostly benefitted larger firms and those able to compete globally. The new policy provides credit and a better framework for small firms.

**4. It pairs openness with security.** The 1991 approach trusted markets almost completely. The newer approach recognises that for sectors important to national security and health, the government should have a plan.

**5. It aims to improve employment.** By promoting labour-intensive manufacturing, it tries to correct the "jobless growth" that critics of 1991 pointed to.

### Where the claim should be questioned

**1. It builds on 1991 and does not replace it.** The growth, global links and investment of the last three decades made Atmanirbhar Bharat possible. India's ability to run PLI schemes depends on the open, competitive economy created in 1991.

**2. Risks of going back to old habits.** Critics worry that higher tariffs, import restrictions and subsidies for chosen industries resemble the pre-1991 import substitution model, which led to high-cost, low-quality industry. Whether the new policy avoids this depends on whether firms are pushed to become competitive and to export, or are simply protected.

**3. Supplier networks.** Modern manufacturing relies on global supply chains. Raising import duties on components can raise costs for Indian manufacturers and make them less competitive in exports. Real self-reliance means being useful to global supply chains, not leaving them.

**4. Costs and results are still uncertain.** Incentive schemes cost public money, and their benefits, such as jobs and exports, take years to appear and must be measured honestly. Some sectors, such as mobile phone assembly, have shown progress, but in others the results are mixed. Much of the early growth in assembly still relies on imported parts, so the value actually added in India may be smaller than it appears. The overall share of manufacturing in the economy has not yet changed dramatically.

**5. Some reforms were not completed.** The farm laws were withdrawn in 2021 after protests, and implementation of other reforms has varied among states.

**6. The situations differ.** The 1991 policy responded to an emergency in which India had no choice. Atmanirbhar Bharat responds to a long-term concern. This makes direct comparison difficult, and "better" depends on what is being measured.

A balanced view is that the 1991 policy opened India to the world and Atmanirbhar Bharat tries to make sure that India benefits from that opening by becoming a stronger producer. Together, they are best seen as two stages of one long process. Whether the second stage turns out to be an improvement will depend on whether it makes Indian firms more competitive, not just more protected.

## Conclusion

The New Economic Policy of 1991 rescued India from a financial crisis by reducing licensing and state control, welcoming private and foreign investment and opening trade. It raised growth and ended the shortage economy, but it left weaknesses in manufacturing, employment and import dependence.

Atmanirbhar Bharat tries to fix these weaknesses by building domestic manufacturing, supporting small firms, reducing dependence on imports for critical goods and strengthening supply chains. It is an improvement in the sense that it targets the gaps that 1991 left, but it depends on the 1991 foundation and carries the risk of returning to excessive protection. Its success will be judged by whether it creates jobs and competitive industries, which can only be known over the coming years.
