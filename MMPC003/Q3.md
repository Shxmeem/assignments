# Question

Describe the New Economic Policy (1991). How is Atmanirbhar Bharat policy an improvement over policy of 1991?

# Introduction

In 1991, India changed the way it ran its economy. The country moved away from heavy government control and opened itself to private business and the world. Nearly three decades later, in 2020, the government announced the Atmanirbhar Bharat policy, which aims at making India self-reliant. This assignment describes the New Economic Policy of 1991 and then explains how Atmanirbhar Bharat can be seen as an improvement over it.

# The New Economic Policy of 1991

**Background.** Before 1991, India followed a system in which the government controlled most of the economy. Industries needed licences to start or grow, imports were tightly restricted and the public sector held the main industries. By 1990-91, the economy was in trouble. India had borrowed heavily, the fiscal deficit was high, prices were rising and the foreign exchange reserves had fallen so low that they could pay for only a few weeks of imports. The rise in oil prices after the Gulf War made it worse. India had to pledge gold to raise money and seek help from the International Monetary Fund, which came with conditions for reform.

The policy was built on three main ideas, commonly called **liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation**.

1. **Liberalisation.** Industrial licensing was removed for most industries, except a short list of those related to security, safety and the environment. Limits on the growth of large companies were relaxed, and many industries earlier kept for the public sector were opened to private firms. Controls on prices and production were reduced. In the financial sector, banks were given more freedom, interest rates were eased and the capital market was opened to more players. Tax reforms lowered the rates and made them simpler.

2. **Privatisation.** The government reduced its role in business. Some public sector units were opened to private ownership by selling part of the government's shares, which is called disinvestment. The aim was to raise money, improve efficiency and let the government focus on areas like education, health and defence.

3. **Globalisation.** India opened its doors to the world. Import duties were brought down, and many import licences were removed. The rupee was devalued and later allowed to move with the market. Foreign companies were allowed to invest, with automatic approval for a share of ownership in many industries. The aim was to bring in money, technology and competition.

**Results.** The policy brought quick changes. The balance of payments crisis eased and reserves grew. The economy grew faster, foreign investment came in, and sectors such as information technology, telecom and banking expanded. Consumers had more choice and better quality. Poverty fell over time. However, the gains were uneven. Services grew much faster than manufacturing, farm income lagged, and many small businesses struggled against imports and large firms. India also became more dependent on imports for goods such as electronics, machinery and medicines.

# Atmanirbhar Bharat

The Atmanirbhar Bharat campaign was announced in May 2020, during the Covid-19 lockdown, along with a large financial package. Its idea is that India should rely on its own strength to produce and to meet its needs, while staying connected to the world. It is built on **five pillars**: the economy, infrastructure, the system of rules, the young population and demand. Some of its main steps were:

- Production linked incentives, which reward companies for making goods in India, in areas such as mobile phones, medicines, solar equipment and electronics.
- Support for small businesses, including a new definition of small firms, loans without collateral and payment of dues owed by government bodies.
- Opening of new areas to private firms, such as coal mining, defence production and space, and a higher limit for foreign investment in defence.
- Lists of defence items that will not be imported, to push local production.
- Encouragement to buy local products, under the call to be **vocal for local**.
- Reforms in farm marketing and storage, and a fund for farm infrastructure.

# How Atmanirbhar Bharat Is an Improvement Over 1991

1. **It fills the gap in manufacturing.** The 1991 reforms helped services much more than factories. Atmanirbhar Bharat targets manufacturing directly, through incentives and support, so that India makes more of what it uses and creates more jobs for ordinary workers.

2. **It reduces dependence on imports.** Opening to imports in 1991 gave choice, but left India reliant on other countries for many key items. The newer policy aims at reducing this, and the shortages seen in the pandemic, when supply chains broke, showed why this matters.

3. **It supports small businesses.** The 1991 reforms favoured larger firms that could compete with imports. The new policy gives direct help to small and medium firms, which provide a large share of the jobs.

4. **It balances openness with national interest.** The 1991 policy was mainly about opening up. Atmanirbhar Bharat keeps trade and foreign investment but adds that the country should build its own strength first, particularly in sensitive areas like defence.

5. **It gives attention to farm and rural areas.** Reforms in 1991 touched agriculture little. The new policy includes steps for farm markets, storage and rural infrastructure.

It should be added that Atmanirbhar Bharat **builds on** 1991 and does not reject it. It is an improvement in direction, not a replacement, and its success depends on how well the steps are carried out.

# Conclusion

The New Economic Policy of 1991 rescued India from a crisis by opening up the economy through liberalisation, privatisation and globalisation, though its gains were uneven. Atmanirbhar Bharat tries to correct these gaps by strengthening manufacturing, small businesses and local production, while keeping India open to the world. Whether it proves a true improvement will depend on its results in the coming years.
