# Goods and Services Tax (GST) in India

## Introduction

Before 2017, a product in India could be taxed many times on its way to the customer. The Centre charged excise duty and service tax, and each state charged VAT, entry tax, octroi and other levies. Each tax was added on top of the one before it. GST was introduced to replace this tangle with one tax on the supply of goods and services across the whole country. It came into force on 1 July 2017, after the 101st Constitutional Amendment.

## What is GST?

GST is an indirect tax charged on the supply of goods and services. It is a **destination-based** tax, which means the tax goes to the state where the goods or services are consumed and not where they are produced. It is also a **multi-stage** tax, collected at every step from manufacturer to retailer. But it is charged only on the **value added** at each step, because every seller can claim back the tax already paid on their purchases. This claim is called **input tax credit**. The final burden falls on the consumer.

*Example:* A manufacturer sells goods worth ₹100 to a wholesaler and charges 18% GST, which is ₹18. The wholesaler adds ₹20 of value and sells for ₹120, charging ₹21.60 GST. The wholesaler pays the government only ₹3.60, which is ₹21.60 minus the ₹18 already paid. In this way, tax is paid only on the ₹20 that was added, and there is no tax on tax.

## Salient Features of GST

**1. One tax for the whole country.** GST replaces many central and state taxes, such as central excise duty, service tax, VAT, entry tax, luxury tax and various cesses. Petroleum products, crude oil and natural gas, alcohol for human consumption and electricity are still outside GST. Alcohol and petroleum continue to be taxed under older state and central levies.

**2. Dual structure.** Both the Centre and the states charge GST on the same supply. There are three components:
- **CGST** (Central GST), collected by the Centre on sales within a state.
- **SGST** (State GST), collected by the state on sales within the state.
- **IGST** (Integrated GST), collected by the Centre on sales between states and on imports, and then shared with the destination state.

For a sale within one state, the tax is split equally between CGST and SGST. For example, 18% GST becomes 9% CGST and 9% SGST.

**3. Destination-based.** Tax revenue goes to the state of consumption. This is a major change from the older system, in which producing states gained the most.

**4. Input tax credit.** Tax paid on inputs can be set off against tax on output. This removes the cascading effect of tax on tax, and is the central idea of GST.

**5. GST Council.** This is a joint body of the Union Finance Minister, the Union Minister of State for Finance and the finance ministers of all states. It decides tax rates, exemptions, thresholds and rules. Decisions need a three-fourths majority of the weighted votes, in which the Centre has one-third and all states together have two-thirds. This makes cooperative federalism a feature of the system.

**6. Simplified rate structure.** GST began in 2017 with four main rates of 5%, 12%, 18% and 28%, along with a cess on some goods. In September 2025, the GST Council approved a simpler structure, which took effect on 22 September 2025. The main rates are now **5% and 18%**, with a special **40% rate** for luxury and "sin" goods, and some items remain at nil or exempt. Most goods earlier taxed at 12% moved to 5%, and most of those earlier taxed at 28% moved to 18%. Some basic items such as unbranded food grains and a number of essential goods are exempt.

**7. Common registration and returns online.** Businesses register under one system, the GST Network (GSTN), and file returns and pay tax online. Small businesses below a turnover limit need not register, and a **composition scheme** allows small traders to pay a low flat rate and file fewer returns.

**8. Tax on every supply, with few exceptions.** GST applies to sale, transfer, barter, rental and import of goods and services, whether or not they are made for profit, as part of business.

**9. No tax on exports.** Exports are zero-rated, so exporters can get back the tax paid on their inputs. Imports are charged IGST, which puts imported and domestic goods on an equal footing.

**10. Anti-profiteering and e-invoicing rules.** Provisions exist to make sure that businesses pass tax benefits on to consumers, and larger businesses must generate electronic invoices, which helps catch fake billing.

## Advantages of GST

**1. Removes cascading of taxes.** Under the old system, tax was charged on a price that already contained earlier taxes. With input credit, tax is paid only on value added, which lowers the cost of goods.

**2. One national market.** Earlier, goods moving between states faced check posts, entry taxes and varied rates, which delayed trucks and raised costs. GST removed state border taxes and most check posts, so goods move faster and over longer distances in a day. Logistics costs fall, and businesses can plan supply chains for the whole country instead of state by state.

**3. Simpler and more transparent.** One tax replaces many, with common rules and online procedures. Consumers can see the tax on the bill, and businesses spend less time dealing with several tax departments.

**4. Wider tax base and better compliance.** Input credit gives businesses a reason to buy only from registered suppliers and ask for invoices, because without a valid invoice they cannot claim credit. This pulls more businesses into the tax net and makes evasion harder. Online matching of invoices across buyers and sellers makes cheating easier to detect. GST collections have risen over the years, reaching new highs, which has strengthened government finances.

**5. Lower prices for many goods.** When the tax burden falls and cascading ends, prices can come down. The 2025 rate cuts, for example, lowered the rate on many everyday items, from packaged food to small vehicles and appliances, and were intended to make them cheaper.

**6. Boost to manufacturing and "Make in India."** Because tax paid on machinery, raw materials and services can be set off, the cost of producing goods in India falls. Exporters get refunds of the tax on their inputs, which makes Indian products more competitive abroad.

**7. Benefits for the economy.** A simple, unified tax system makes the country more attractive to investors, increases formalisation of the economy and can raise growth over time.

**8. Cooperative federalism.** The GST Council gives states a real voice in tax decisions, instead of the Centre alone deciding. The states received protection against revenue loss in the early years through a compensation arrangement.

**9. Less burden on small businesses.** Small traders below the threshold need not register, and those who are in the composition scheme pay a small tax and file simpler returns.

## Limitations and Criticisms

For a balanced answer, it is fair to note that GST has not been perfect.

- Even after the 2025 reform, there are still several rates and a number of exceptions, and disputes over classification continue.
- Petroleum, alcohol and electricity remain outside GST, so some tax cascading continues in these sectors.
- Compliance can be heavy for small businesses, particularly in the early years, with frequent returns and technical problems on the portal.
- States have worried about losing revenue and about their reduced freedom to set their own taxes. Rate cuts reduce collections for both the Centre and states.
- Delays in refunds and in input tax credit matching have strained the cash flow of some businesses, especially exporters.
- Rate changes make it necessary for businesses to adjust their systems and prices repeatedly.

## Conclusion

GST is a destination-based tax on the supply of goods and services, collected at every stage but charged only on value added, and shared between the Centre and the states. Its main features are one nation-wide tax, dual administration through CGST, SGST and IGST, input tax credit, a Council that decides jointly and a simplified rate structure, now centred on 5% and 18% with a higher rate for luxury and sin goods. Its main advantages are the end of tax on tax, a single national market, simpler and more transparent compliance, a wider tax base and support for manufacturing and exports. It still has gaps, such as items kept outside it and compliance difficulties, but it is widely seen as the most important tax reform since independence.
