# Beyond Pay: Rewards and Empowerment in a Global Organisation

## Introduction

Pay is the first thing most people look at when choosing a job, and it is the first thing they complain about when they are unhappy. So it is easy to assume that better pay solves most problems of motivation and turnover. Yet many organisations have raised salaries and still lost good people, and many employees stay in lower-paying jobs because they like the work and the people.

This assignment evaluates the statement that pay alone is not enough, looking at changing expectations and a more varied workforce. It then designs a reward and empowerment strategy for a global organisation that wants higher productivity, more inclusion and a stronger sense of ownership among its employees.

## Evaluating the Statement

**Where the statement is right.** Herzberg's research is the best-known support. He found that pay and working conditions mainly prevent unhappiness, but do not by themselves create motivation. Motivation came from interesting work, a sense of achievement, recognition and responsibility. Self-determination theory points the same way: people work best when they have some choice, feel capable and feel connected to others. Money can supply none of these directly. There is also a practical problem. A pay rise is quickly accepted as normal, and the pleasure fades within months, while a competitor can always offer a little more. A company that competes only on pay is always one offer away from losing its people.

**Changing expectations.** Employees today expect more from work than their parents did. Many want flexibility in where and when they work, a chance to learn new skills, work that feels meaningful and a manager who treats them with respect. Younger employees often move jobs sooner if they feel they are not growing. Mental health and balance between work and life have also become normal topics, and people are more willing to leave a well-paid job that harms them. In knowledge work, people also know that their skills are valued elsewhere, so they have more choice.

**Workforce diversity.** A global organisation has employees of different ages, cultures, family situations and life stages, and they want different things. A young employee may want fast growth and learning. A parent may value flexible hours and predictable schedules. An older employee may value respect for experience and secure retirement benefits. An employee in a country with weak public services may value health insurance more than a bonus. Employees from groups that have faced discrimination may care most about being treated fairly and having an equal chance of promotion. A single reward package cannot suit all of them, which supports the statement further.

**Where the statement goes too far.** Pay still matters, and in some situations it matters most. If people cannot cover basic needs, no amount of recognition or meaningful work will keep them. This is true in many low-income settings and for many lower-paid workers around the world. Unfair pay is also a strong cause of anger. If people find that colleagues doing the same work earn more for no clear reason, trust is damaged quickly. So the correct reading is that fair and competitive pay is a necessary base, but it is not enough on its own. Pay gets people in the door and keeps them from leaving angry. What makes them put in their best effort and stay for years is usually something else.

## Proposed Strategy: The Base, Choice, Ownership Strategy

The strategy has three layers. Each layer deals with a different need, and all three are needed.

**1. Base: fair and clear pay.**

- *Pay that is fair and competitive.* Pay is set using local market information in each country, so that it is competitive where the person lives and not simply a copy of headquarters' rates.
- *Equal pay for equal work.* The organisation checks regularly whether people doing similar work are paid differently because of gender, nationality, age or other non-work reasons, and corrects gaps it finds.
- *Open pay rules.* Employees are told how pay ranges are set and what is needed to move up. Secrecy produces suspicion and rumours, while clear rules build trust.
- *Basic security.* Every employee in every country has at least basic health cover, fair leave and protection against sudden loss of income, adjusted to what is normal and useful locally.

**2. Choice: rewards that fit different people.**

- *A menu of benefits.* Beyond the base, employees choose from options that suit them within a set budget, such as extra health cover for family, childcare support, learning funds, additional leave, retirement contributions or flexible hours. This respects the fact that people have different needs at different stages of life.
- *Flexible work.* Where the work allows, employees have a say in where and when they work, and the rules are applied equally to everyone.
- *Recognition that is specific.* Managers are trained to acknowledge good work promptly and specifically, saying what was done and why it mattered. Peers can also recognise each other. Recognition costs little and, when it is sincere and specific, is valued highly.
- *Growth as a reward.* Training, interesting projects, mentoring and moves to other teams or countries are treated as rewards that can be earned and requested, not as favours.
- *Team rewards alongside individual ones.* Part of the reward depends on team or company results, so cooperation is rewarded and not only personal achievement.
- *Local meaning.* Recognition is adapted to local culture. Public praise is welcome in some places and embarrassing in others, so employees are asked how they prefer to be recognised.

**3. Ownership: empowerment and a real stake.**

- *Real decision authority.* Teams decide how to reach their goals, within clear limits on budget and risk, instead of asking for approval at every step. The limits are written down so people know what they can decide alone.
- *A voice in matters that affect them.* Employees are consulted on changes to their work, and shown how their input was used. If it was not used, they are given a reason.
- *Time and money for ideas.* Employees can propose improvements and, if approved, receive time and a small budget to test them. Those who contribute are credited by name.
- *Sharing in the results.* Where possible, employees share in the organisation's success through profit sharing or share ownership, in a form suited to each country's laws and customs. Even a small stake helps people see the company's results as partly their own.
- *Inclusive participation.* The organisation checks who actually speaks in meetings, gets chosen for projects and gets promoted, and takes steps when some groups are left out. This includes giving employees in different countries and time zones equal chances to take part. Managers are trained to invite quieter people and to handle language differences with patience.
- *Leaders who explain.* Leaders share the reasons behind decisions and the financial position of the company in plain terms, so employees can understand how their work connects to the whole.

**How the layers work together.** The base removes worry and unfairness, so people can think about their work rather than their bills. The choice layer shows that the organisation sees each person as an individual and not as a number. The ownership layer gives people the authority and the stake to care about outcomes. If the base is weak, the other layers feel like decoration. If only the base exists, people are satisfied but not committed. Together they address the practical, personal and shared parts of motivation.

## How the Strategy Improves Productivity, Inclusion and Ownership

**Productivity.** People work harder and better when they have authority, clear goals and recognition. Decisions are made closer to the work, which saves time. Learning and flexible arrangements reduce turnover and absence, which saves the large hidden cost of replacing people. Ideas from employees often show practical savings that managers cannot see from above.

**Inclusion.** Pay equity checks, a choice of benefits and fair rules on flexible work address the real differences in people's situations. Checking who takes part and who is promoted turns inclusion from a statement into something that can be measured and corrected. Employees who see that the organisation acts on unfairness are more likely to trust it.

**Psychological ownership.** People feel that something is theirs when they have control over it, understand it well and have invested effort in it. The strategy provides all three: authority over their work, openness about how the company operates and the chance to put their own ideas into practice. Sharing in results adds a financial reason as well. Employees who feel ownership look after quality, spend resources carefully and tend to stay.

## Limitations

The strategy is costly and complex. Different benefit menus and local pay setting need administration, which may be difficult for a global company with many legal systems. Empowerment can fail if managers who are used to control take back decisions in practice, or if people are given authority without training or information. Sharing ownership is not possible everywhere and may expose employees to risk if the company does poorly. Recognition that feels forced or repeated loses its value quickly. There is also a risk of unfair treatment in the choice layer, if some groups quietly receive better options than others. Finally, the results are slow to appear, and a company under pressure may cut the non-pay elements first, which would remove what makes the strategy work. It is sensible to start with a few countries, measure the effects on turnover and engagement, and expand step by step.

## Conclusion

The statement is largely correct. Pay is a necessary base, but people today, with their varied backgrounds and higher expectations, are motivated and retained by meaningful work, growth, respect, flexibility and a say in decisions. Still, pay cannot be ignored, especially where incomes are low or pay is seen as unfair.

The Base, Choice, Ownership strategy begins with fair pay, adds rewards that fit different people and gives employees real authority and a stake in results. It is meant to raise productivity, make inclusion measurable and build the sense that the work belongs to those who do it. It will work only if managers are willing to share control and the organisation keeps up the effort when money is tight.
