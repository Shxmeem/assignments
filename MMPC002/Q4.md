# Introduction

Pay is the first thing most people look at when they join a job, and it is the first thing they compare when they think of leaving. Yet many organisations that pay well still lose good people and still see weak effort. This assignment looks at whether pay alone is enough to motivate and keep employees today, considering what employees now expect and how varied the workforce has become. It then sets out a reward and empowerment plan for a global organisation, aimed at better productivity, inclusion and a sense of ownership among employees.

# Evaluation of the Statement

The statement is **largely correct**, but it needs some care. Pay still matters. If it is unfair or too low, nothing else will make up for it, and people will leave or stop trying. So pay is necessary. The question is whether it is enough, and the answer is no.

Expectations have changed in several ways. Earlier, a steady job with a fixed career path was seen as a good deal. Now many employees, especially younger ones, expect to learn quickly, work on meaningful tasks, get regular feedback and have some say in how they work. Flexible hours and the option to work from home are no longer treated as favours. Many people also want their employer to treat people fairly and to care about more than profit. When these are missing, a good salary does not hold people for long, because a similar salary is easy to find elsewhere.

Pay also has a weakness as a motivator. People get used to a raise within a few months and then expect the next one. Pay can make people complete a task, but it does little to make them think about the task, suggest improvements or stay when things get hard. Rewards tied closely to targets can even push people to chase the number and ignore the quality of work or the needs of colleagues.

Workforce diversity makes the problem bigger. A global organisation has employees of different ages, cultures, genders, family situations and abilities. A young engineer may want fast promotion and learning. A parent may want flexible hours more than a bonus. An older employee may value respect for experience and a calmer pace. An employee with a disability may need changes to the workplace before any reward has meaning. In some countries, job security and family benefits count most, while in others, personal growth and independence matter more. One pay package cannot suit all these people. A reward that one group likes may feel useless or even unfair to another.

So the statement holds. Pay keeps people from leaving for the wrong reasons, but it does not give them a reason to give their best or to stay for the right ones.

# Reward and Empowerment Strategy for a Global Organisation

The strategy has five parts. Each part is meant to support productivity, inclusion and **psychological ownership**, which means employees feeling that the work and the organisation are partly theirs.

1. **Fair and open pay.** The base is a pay structure that is clear and checked every year for gaps between men and women and between groups doing similar work. Pay ranges are set by local conditions, but the rules behind them are the same everywhere and are explained to employees. When people understand how pay is decided, they trust it, and pay stops being a source of complaint. A part of the pay can be linked to team results rather than only individual results, so that people gain by helping each other.

2. **Choice in rewards.** Beyond pay, employees choose from a set of benefits that fit their lives, such as extra leave, childcare or elder care support, health cover, learning budgets, flexible hours or help with study fees. Each person picks what matters most to them. This respects differences in age, culture and family situation without singling anyone out. Local teams can add or remove options based on what employees in that country need.

3. **Growth and learning.** Every employee has a learning budget and time set aside for it, along with a talk with the manager about the next role. Opportunities for projects, transfers across countries and mentoring are open to everyone through a clear process, and not only to those whom managers already know. This is also important for inclusion, since people who are outside the main office or the main group often miss such chances.

4. **Real say in the work.** Employees are given authority over how they do their tasks, within clear limits. Teams plan their own work, solve problems on the spot and can spend a small budget without waiting for several approvals. Employees are asked for their views before decisions that affect them, and they are told what was done with their suggestions. Where possible, employees can own part of the result through profit sharing or share options, because owning a stake makes people think like owners.

5. **Recognition and a fair workplace.** Managers thank people for specific work in a timely way, and colleagues can recognise each other. Recognition covers effort, teamwork and ideas, and not only sales or output. Managers are trained to listen and to treat people from different backgrounds fairly. Employees can raise problems without fear, and the organisation acts on what it hears. Meetings are scheduled with different time zones and languages in mind, so that people far from the head office are not left out.

To know whether the plan works, the organisation can track staff turnover, output per team, the spread of promotions and pay across groups, and the replies to a short yearly survey on trust, fairness and whether people feel their work matters. The results are shared with employees and used to adjust the plan.

# Conclusion

Pay is needed but cannot do the whole job, because employees now want growth, flexibility, respect and a voice, and different groups want different things. A plan that combines fair pay, a choice of rewards, learning, real authority and honest recognition can raise productivity, make people feel included and give them a sense of ownership in their work.
