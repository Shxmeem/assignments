# From Personnel Administration to Strategic Partner: The Changing Role of HRM

## Introduction

Not long ago, the HR department was the place that kept records, issued appointment letters, calculated salaries and made sure rules were followed. It supported the business but was rarely part of its important decisions. Today, many organisations expect HR to help decide who to hire, which skills to build, how to keep good people and how to organise work when technology and competition keep changing.

This assignment evaluates how far that change has actually happened and where it has fallen short. It then proposes an HRM framework for organisations that use AI, compete across countries and have employees working partly from home and partly from the office.

## Critical Evaluation of the Transformation

**The traditional role.** Early personnel management was administrative. Its job was to hire, pay, record attendance, handle disputes and keep the organisation within the law. People were treated as a cost to be controlled. The work was necessary, but HR was seen as a support function with little influence on strategy.

**Why the role changed.** Several developments pushed HR to change. As work became more knowledge-based, the quality of employees began to decide how well a firm performed. Researchers such as Ulrich argued that HR should act as a strategic partner, an administrative expert, an employee champion and a change agent. The resource-based view of the firm added the idea that skilled, hard-to-copy people are a source of lasting advantage. Competition for talent, new laws and rapid technology change made it harder to treat HR as only a back office.

**What has improved.** In many companies HR leaders now sit in senior management meetings. HR is involved in workforce planning, in mergers and restructuring, and in building leadership. Data on hiring, attrition and performance is used to support decisions, rather than relying only on instinct.

**Where the claim is exaggerated.** The idea that HR has become a true strategic partner is often more a statement than a fact. In many organisations, especially smaller or family-run ones, HR is still mostly administrative. Where HR has a seat at the table, it is often asked to carry out decisions that have already been made, such as layoffs, instead of helping to shape them. Some HR professionals lack the business and financial knowledge needed to be taken seriously by other managers. There is also a real tension. HR is expected to serve management's goals and also to protect employees' interests. Being a "business partner" can slowly push HR to side with the business even when employees are treated unfairly. Finally, the administrative work has not disappeared. If HR neglects payroll accuracy and compliance in order to appear strategic, trust in the whole function is lost.

A fair conclusion is that HR has moved from administration towards strategy, but unevenly. The strategic role is real where leaders give HR genuine authority and HR brings useful knowledge in return. It remains partly a label elsewhere.

## Proposed Framework: The Plan, Support, Protect Framework

The new environment creates three connected demands. AI changes which jobs and skills are needed. Global competition requires people and practices that work across borders. Hybrid work changes how people are managed, connected and treated. The framework answers these with three responsibilities for HR.

**1. Plan: match people to future work.**

- *Skills-based workforce planning.* HR works with business leaders to map which tasks AI will take over, which will change, and which new ones will appear. Planning is done by skill rather than only by job title, so that people can move into new roles as work changes.
- *Continuous learning.* Each employee has a learning plan, with time during work hours set aside for it. Employees whose tasks are being automated are offered retraining first, before outside hiring is considered.
- *Global talent sourcing.* HR looks for talent across countries, but keeps pay and promotion rules clear so that people doing similar work in different places are not treated unfairly without reason.
- *Succession.* Future leaders are identified early and developed through assignments in different teams and countries.

**2. Support: help people work well in a hybrid setting.**

- *Clear hybrid rules.* The organisation decides which work needs to be done together and which can be done remotely. These rules are written down and applied equally, so that people who work from home are not quietly passed over for promotion.
- *Outcome-based management.* Managers are trained to judge people by results rather than by how visible they are. This reduces unfair advantage for those who are physically present.
- *Wellbeing.* HR sets limits on after-hours messages, watches workload and makes mental health support easy to reach. Hybrid and global teams often work long and irregular hours, and the strain goes unnoticed unless someone looks for it.
- *Connection.* Planned in-person meetings, mixed teams across locations and mentoring help keep relationships strong when people rarely meet.
- *Manager skills.* Managers receive training in leading remote teams, giving feedback and handling cultural differences, since most problems in hybrid work come from weak management and not from the technology.

**3. Protect: use AI and data fairly.**

- *Human decisions on people matters.* AI may help screen applications, suggest training or predict who might leave. But decisions on hiring, firing, pay and promotion are reviewed by a person who can explain the reason.
- *Checking for bias.* HR regularly tests whether AI tools treat groups of people differently. Past data often carries past unfairness, and a tool trained on it can repeat it.
- *Privacy and openness.* Employees are told what data is collected, why, and how it is used. Monitoring that goes beyond what the work requires is avoided.
- *Employee voice.* There are working channels for employees to question decisions, raise concerns and give feedback. This includes workers in other countries, who often have the least access to headquarters.
- *Local laws and fairness.* Global policies are adjusted to follow the labour laws and customs of each country, without lowering basic standards of fair treatment.

**How the parts work together.** Planning without support leads to people being retrained but left unsure and exhausted. Support without planning keeps people comfortable but unprepared for change. Both without protection risk using data and technology in ways that employees do not trust, which destroys the cooperation that planning and support depend on. HR's strategic value in this framework comes from keeping all three in balance. The measures of success include how quickly skills gaps are closed, how many people stay, how fairly different groups are treated and how employees rate trust in management, in addition to the usual cost and hiring figures.

**Limits of the framework.** It needs money and managerial time, which smaller firms may lack. It depends on senior leaders who truly give HR a role in decisions. AI tools change quickly, so rules about their use must be reviewed often. Some employees may not want to retrain, and not every job can be saved. HR should be honest about this and support people leaving with fair treatment, not promise what it cannot deliver.

## Conclusion

HRM has moved from keeping records to helping shape how organisations use their people, but this change is incomplete and uneven. In many places the strategic role is still more claimed than real, and HR faces a continuing tension between serving the business and protecting employees.

The Plan, Support, Protect framework is built for an environment of AI, global competition and hybrid work. It asks HR to prepare people for changing work, to help them work well wherever they are, and to make sure technology is used fairly. HR earns its place as a strategic partner not by using the title, but by giving leaders useful knowledge about people while keeping employees' trust.
