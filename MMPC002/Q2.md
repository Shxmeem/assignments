# Fixing Talent Shortages and Turnover in an Expanding Multinational

## Introduction

When a company expands quickly, hiring usually becomes a race to fill empty seats. Managers in new markets ask for people urgently, job descriptions are copied from old ones, interviews are rushed, and new employees are left to figure things out themselves. The result is what this company is seeing: not enough suitable people, people who do not fit the work, and people who leave within a year or two.

This assignment treats these three problems as symptoms of one weakness, which is that hiring and planning are not connected. It proposes a model that links planning, job analysis, recruitment, selection and socialisation into one process, and then justifies how it supports long-term stability and the ability to adapt.

## Diagnosing the Problem

**Talent shortage.** Shortages often come from poor planning rather than from a lack of people in the market. If a company does not know what skills it will need next year, it starts searching only when the need is urgent, and urgent searches are slow and costly. Shortages also grow when a company looks only for ready-made experts and ignores people who could be trained.

**Skill mismatch.** A mismatch usually starts with an inaccurate job description. If nobody has checked what the job actually involves, the company advertises for the wrong skills, interviews for the wrong qualities and hires people who struggle. Across countries it gets worse, because the same job title can mean different work in different places.

**High turnover.** People leave for many reasons, but the most common ones can be traced to the hiring process. Some were promised a job that turned out different. Some were hired for skills they rarely get to use. Some were ignored in their first months and never felt part of the company. These are failures of the process, and they are often blamed on "unreliable employees" when the company should look at itself.

The three problems feed each other. A mismatch causes poor performance and early exits, early exits create shortages, and shortages lead to rushed hiring, which causes more mismatches. The model is built to break this loop.

## The Proposed Model: Plan, Define, Attract and Choose, Settle

The model has four connected stages. The output of each stage is the input of the next, so they must be run as one system and not as separate departmental tasks.

**Stage 1: Plan (human resource planning).**

- *Look ahead.* HR sits with business leaders each year to ask where the company is expanding, which products are growing, and what skills each will need over the next two to three years.
- *Count what exists.* The company keeps a simple record of the skills its current employees have, including skills not used in their present jobs. Many shortages can be partly filled from inside.
- *Find the gap and decide how to close it.* For each gap the company chooses between training current staff, moving people between locations, hiring new people or using outside contractors. Hiring is not the default answer.
- *Watch the leaving rate.* HR tracks where and why people leave, by department, country and length of service, and treats the numbers as a guide for the plan.

**Stage 2: Define (job analysis).**

- *Study the real work.* For important and repeated roles, HR talks to people doing the job and their managers, and watches the work where possible. The aim is to learn what tasks are done, what skills are truly needed, and what conditions the job involves.
- *Separate must-have from nice-to-have.* Each role has a short list of essential skills and a separate list of skills that can be learned on the job. This widens the pool, because the company stops rejecting capable people for missing minor requirements.
- *Use common role descriptions with local changes.* A role has one core description used everywhere, with small adjustments for local conditions. This makes it possible to compare roles and move people between countries.
- *Keep them current.* Descriptions are reviewed when the work changes, at least once a year for fast-changing roles.

**Stage 3: Attract and Choose (recruitment and selection).**

- *Look in more places.* Besides job advertisements, the company builds links with colleges and training institutes, runs internship and apprenticeship programmes, asks employees to refer people, and invites former employees to return. It also looks at people from other industries whose skills transfer.
- *Be honest in advertising.* Job advertisements and interviews describe the work, pay, hours and difficulties as they actually are. This may reduce the number of applicants, but it increases the number who stay.
- *Test what the job needs.* Selection uses practical tasks, structured interviews with the same questions for every candidate, and work samples, instead of relying on casual conversation. Interviewers are trained and asked to score candidates against the essential skills before discussing them together, which reduces personal bias.
- *Judge fit and learning ability.* Besides present skills, the company checks how quickly a person learns, how they work with others, and whether the work and values suit them. A person who learns quickly is often a better long-term choice than one with exactly the right experience today.
- *Keep local hiring local, but fair.* Country teams run their own hiring using the shared standards, so that they understand the local market and also apply the same quality of selection.
- *Check the process.* HR reviews whether the selection tools actually predicted good performance, and whether any group is being rejected without a work-related reason.

**Stage 4: Settle (employee socialisation).**

- *Prepare before day one.* New employees receive clear information about their role, team and first tasks before they join, so that the early weeks are not wasted.
- *Give a structured first period.* For the first three to six months, each person has a plan: who to meet, what to learn, and what is expected by the end of each month. The manager has regular conversations with the new person during this time.
- *Assign a buddy.* An experienced colleague who is not the manager helps with everyday questions. For people moving to a new country, the buddy also helps with practical and cultural matters.
- *Explain how things are done.* The company is clear about its working style, decision-making and values through real examples, and not only through presentations.
- *Check early.* HR contacts new employees at about one, three and six months to ask what is working and what is not. Warning signs found early can be fixed before the person resigns.
- *Show a path.* Employees are shown how they can grow, move to other roles or countries, and learn new skills.

**How the stages connect.** Planning tells the company what to look for. Job analysis turns that into accurate roles. Recruitment and selection find people who match those roles. Socialisation makes sure the people who were chosen actually settle in. The information then flows back: exit data and early feedback show where planning, role descriptions or selection were wrong, and they are corrected. This feedback is what makes the model a loop that improves, not a one-time project.

## Justification: Sustainability and Adaptability

**Long-term sustainability.** The model reduces dependence on emergency hiring. Because planning looks ahead, the company can start building skills before they are urgently needed. Honest recruitment and careful selection mean fewer people leave in the first year, which saves the cost of repeated hiring and training, and keeps knowledge inside the company. Good socialisation helps people form relationships and feel committed, and committed people are more likely to stay. Over time, the company builds a steady supply of people from its own internal development and from its links with schools and colleges, and it depends less on a competitive outside market.

**Workforce adaptability.** The model supports change in three ways. First, it records skills and not only job titles, so the company can see who can move into new work when the business changes. Second, it selects for learning ability, so that new hires can adjust when tasks change. Third, it treats training and internal moves as the first option when a gap appears, which builds people who are used to learning new things. Because role descriptions are reviewed regularly, the company's picture of its own work stays up to date.

**Fairness and trust.** Structured selection, honest information and regular checks for unfair patterns help the company treat people consistently across countries. This protects its reputation, which matters in a market where good candidates compare employers.

## Limitations

The model costs time and money. Job analysis and structured selection are slower than quick hiring, and it may feel hard to justify this during rapid growth. The company can reduce the cost by starting with the roles that are hardest to fill or that have the highest turnover, and by extending later. The model also depends on managers in every country following the process, and on accurate data, and both need training and attention. Some turnover is not caused by hiring at all but by pay, poor managers or better offers elsewhere, and this model alone will not solve those. Finally, forecasts of future skills will sometimes be wrong, so the plan must be reviewed regularly and not treated as fixed.

## Conclusion

Talent shortages, skill mismatches and high turnover in a fast-growing company are not separate problems. They come from planning that does not look ahead, roles that are poorly defined, hiring that is rushed, and new employees who are left without support. Treating them one at a time only moves the problem around.

The model proposed here connects planning, job analysis, recruitment, selection and socialisation, and uses information from each stage to improve the others. It helps the company keep people longer, fill gaps from within where possible, and respond when its needs change. It requires patience, discipline and honest data, but a company that builds its workforce deliberately is in a stronger position than one that is always hiring in a hurry.
