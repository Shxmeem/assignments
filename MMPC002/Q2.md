# Fixing Talent Shortages and Turnover in an Expanding Multinational

## Introduction

When a company grows fast, hiring becomes a race to fill empty seats. Managers in new markets ask for people urgently, job descriptions are copied from old ones, interviews are rushed, and new employees are left to figure things out alone. The result is too few suitable people, poor fit between people and work, and staff who leave within a year or two.

This assignment treats these as symptoms of one weakness: hiring and planning are not connected. It proposes a model linking planning, job analysis, recruitment, selection and socialisation, and explains how it supports long-term stability and adaptability.

## Diagnosing the Problem

**Talent shortage.** Shortages often come from poor planning, not a lack of people in the market. If the company does not know what skills it needs next year, it searches only when the need is urgent, which is slow and costly. Shortages also grow when a company looks only for ready-made experts and ignores people who could be trained.

**Skill mismatch.** This usually starts with an inaccurate job description. If nobody has checked what the job really involves, the company advertises for the wrong skills, interviews for the wrong qualities and hires people who struggle. Across countries it gets worse, as the same job title can mean different work in different places.

**High turnover.** Many exits trace back to hiring: a job that turned out different from the promise, skills that are rarely used, or a new employee ignored in the first months and never made to feel part of the company. These are failures of the process, yet they are often blamed on "unreliable employees".

These problems feed each other. Mismatch causes poor performance and early exits, exits create shortages, and shortages cause rushed hiring and more mismatch. The model is built to break this loop.

## The Proposed Model: Plan, Define, Attract and Choose, Settle

Each stage feeds the next, so they must run as one system, not as separate departmental tasks.

**Stage 1: Plan (human resource planning)**
- *Look ahead.* Each year, HR sits with business leaders to ask where the company is expanding and what skills each area will need over the next two to three years.
- *Count what exists.* Keep a simple record of employees' skills, including ones not used in their present jobs. Many gaps can be partly filled from inside.
- *Close the gap.* For each gap, choose between training current staff, moving people between locations, hiring or using contractors. Hiring is not the default.
- *Watch leaving rates.* Track where and why people leave, by department, country and length of service, and use the numbers to guide the plan.

**Stage 2: Define (job analysis)**
- *Study the real work.* Talk to people doing key roles and their managers, and observe the work where possible, to learn the real tasks, skills and conditions.
- *Must-have versus nice-to-have.* Keep a short list of essential skills and a separate list of skills that can be learned on the job. This widens the pool, because capable people are no longer rejected for minor gaps.
- *One core description, local changes.* This lets the company compare roles and move people between countries.
- *Keep it current.* Review descriptions when the work changes, at least yearly for fast-changing roles.

**Stage 3: Attract and Choose (recruitment and selection)**
- *Look in more places.* Use colleges, training institutes, internships, apprenticeships, employee referrals, returning ex-employees and people from other industries whose skills transfer.
- *Be honest.* Describe the work, pay, hours and difficulties as they are. Fewer people may apply, but more will stay.
- *Test what the job needs.* Use work samples and structured interviews with the same questions for everyone. Train interviewers to score candidates against the essential skills before discussing them together, which reduces personal bias.
- *Judge learning ability and fit.* Check how quickly a person learns, how they work with others, and whether the work and values suit them. A fast learner is often a better long-term choice than someone with exactly the right experience today.
- *Local hiring, shared standards.* Country teams hire locally because they know their market, but use common standards so selection quality stays equal.
- *Check the process.* Review whether the tools predicted good performance and whether any group is rejected without a work-related reason.

**Stage 4: Settle (socialisation)**
Share the role, team and first tasks before day one. Give each person a three-to-six-month plan with regular manager talks. Assign a buddy, not the manager, for everyday and cultural questions. HR checks in at one, three and six months to fix problems early.

**How the stages connect.** Planning shows what to look for, job analysis defines roles, recruitment and selection find matching people, and socialisation helps them settle. Exit data and early feedback then flow back to correct earlier stages, so the model is a loop, not a one-time project.

**Justification.** Planning ahead reduces emergency hiring. Honest recruitment means fewer first-year exits, saving hiring and training costs. Recording skills, not just titles, lets the company move people into new work when the business changes.

**Limitations.** It costs time and money and is slower than quick hiring. It relies on managers in every country following the process, and it cannot fix pay or poor managers. Skill forecasts will sometimes be wrong, so plans need review.

**Conclusion.** Talent shortages, skill mismatches and high turnover come from weak planning, vague roles, rushed hiring and little support for new staff. This model links each stage and uses feedback to improve the others. A company that builds its workforce deliberately is stronger than one always hiring in a hurry.
