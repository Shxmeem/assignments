# Fixing Talent Shortages and Turnover in an Expanding Multinational

## Introduction

When a company expands quickly, hiring becomes a race to fill empty seats. Managers in new markets ask for people urgently, job descriptions are copied from old ones, interviews are rushed, and new employees are left to figure things out alone. The result is what this company is seeing: too few suitable people, people who do not fit the work, and people who leave within a year or two.

This assignment treats these as symptoms of one weakness: hiring and planning are not connected. It proposes a model that links planning, job analysis, recruitment, selection and socialisation into one process, and justifies how it supports stability and adaptability.

## Diagnosing the Problem

**Talent shortage.**  
Shortages often come from poor planning, not from a lack of people in the market. If the company does not know what skills it needs next year, it starts searching only when the need is urgent, and urgent searches are slow and costly. It also looks only for ready-made experts and ignores people who could be trained.

**Skill mismatch.**  
This usually starts with an inaccurate job description. If nobody has checked what the job involves, the company advertises for the wrong skills and hires people who struggle. Across countries it gets worse, because the same job title can mean different work in different places.

**High turnover.**  
Many exits can be traced to hiring. Some people were promised a job that turned out different. Some were hired for skills they rarely use. Some were ignored in their first months and never felt part of the company. These are process failures, but they are often blamed on "unreliable employees".

The three problems feed each other. Mismatch causes poor performance and early exits, exits create shortages, and shortages lead to rushed hiring and more mismatches. The model is built to break this loop.

## The Proposed Model: Plan, Define, Attract and Choose, Settle

The model has four connected stages. The output of each is the input of the next, so they run as one system, not as separate departmental tasks.

### Stage 1: Plan (human resource planning)

- *Look ahead.* Each year HR sits with business leaders to ask where the company is expanding and what skills it will need over the next two to three years.
- *Count what exists.* The company keeps a simple record of employees' skills, including those not used in their present jobs. Many gaps can be partly filled from inside.
- *Close the gap.* For each gap the company chooses between training, moving people between locations, hiring or using contractors. Hiring is not the default.
- *Watch the leaving rate.* HR tracks where and why people leave, by department, country and length of service, and uses the numbers to guide the plan.

### Stage 2: Define (job analysis)

- *Study the real work.* For important roles, HR talks to people doing the job and their managers, and watches the work where possible, to learn the tasks, the skills truly needed and the conditions.
- *Separate must-have from nice-to-have.* Each role has a short list of essential skills and another list of skills that can be learned on the job. This widens the pool, because capable people are no longer rejected for minor gaps.
- *Use common descriptions with local changes.* A role has one core description used everywhere, with small local adjustments. This allows comparison and moves between countries.
- *Keep them current.* Descriptions are reviewed when the work changes, at least yearly for fast-changing roles.

### Stage 3: Attract and Choose (recruitment and selection)

- *Look in more places.* Besides advertisements, the company builds links with colleges and training institutes, runs internships and apprenticeships, uses employee referrals, invites former employees back, and looks at people from other industries whose skills transfer.
- *Be honest.* Advertisements and interviews describe the work, pay, hours and difficulties as they are. This may reduce applicants but increases the number who stay.
- *Test what the job needs.* Selection uses practical tasks, work samples and structured interviews with the same questions for every candidate. Interviewers are trained to score candidates against the essential skills before discussing them together, which reduces personal bias.
- *Judge fit and learning ability.* The company checks how quickly a person learns, how they work with others, and whether the work and values suit them. A fast learner is often a better long-term choice than someone with exactly the right experience today.
- *Keep hiring local but fair.* Country teams run their own hiring using shared standards, so they understand the local market while keeping the same quality of selection.
- *Check the process.* HR reviews whether the selection tools predicted good performance, and whether any group is rejected without a work-related reason.

### Stage 4: Settle (employee socialisation)

- *Prepare before day one.* New employees receive clear information about their role, team and first tasks before joining.
- *Give a structured first period.* For the first three to six months each person has a plan: who to meet, what to learn, and what is expected each month, with regular conversations with the manager.
- *Assign a buddy.* An experienced colleague, not the manager, helps with everyday questions. For people moving countries, the buddy also helps with practical and cultural matters.
- *Explain how things are done.* The company shows its working style, decision-making and values through real examples, not only presentations.
- *Check early.* HR contacts new employees at about one, three and six months. Warning signs found early can be fixed before the person resigns.
- *Show a path.* Employees see how they can grow, move to other roles or countries, and learn new skills.

**How the stages connect.** Planning tells the company what to look for. Job analysis turns that into accurate roles. Recruitment and selection find people who match them. Socialisation makes sure those people settle in. Information then flows back: exit data and early feedback show where planning, role descriptions or selection went wrong, and these are corrected. This feedback makes the model a loop that improves, not a one-time project.

## Justification: Sustainability and Adaptability

**Long-term sustainability.** The model reduces dependence on emergency hiring. Because planning looks ahead, the company can build skills before they are urgently needed. Honest recruitment and careful selection mean fewer first-year exits, which saves repeated hiring and training costs and keeps knowledge inside the company. Good socialisation helps people form relationships and feel committed, and committed people stay longer. Over time the company builds a steady supply of people from internal development and links with schools and colleges, and depends less on a competitive outside market.

**Workforce adaptability.** The model supports change in three ways. It records skills, not only job titles, so the company can see who can move into new work. It selects for learning ability, so new hires can adjust when tasks change. And it treats training and internal moves as the first option when a gap appears, which builds people used to learning new things. Regular review of role descriptions keeps the company's picture of its own work up to date.

**Fairness and trust.** Structured selection, honest information and regular checks for unfair patterns help the company treat people consistently across countries. This protects its reputation, which matters when good candidates compare employers.

## Limitations

The model costs time and money. Job analysis and structured selection are slower than quick hiring, which is hard to justify during rapid growth. The company can limit the cost by starting with the hardest-to-fill or highest-turnover roles and extending later. The model also depends on managers in every country following the process and on accurate data, and both need training and attention. Some turnover is caused by pay, poor managers or better offers elsewhere, and this model alone will not solve that. Finally, forecasts of future skills will sometimes be wrong, so the plan must be reviewed regularly and not treated as fixed.

## Conclusion

Talent shortages, skill mismatches and high turnover in a fast-growing company are not separate problems. They come from planning that does not look ahead, poorly defined roles, rushed hiring and new employees left without support. Treating them one at a time only moves the problem around.

The proposed model connects planning, job analysis, recruitment, selection and socialisation, and uses information from each stage to improve the others. It helps the company keep people longer, fill gaps from within where possible, and respond when its needs change. It requires patience, discipline and honest data, but a company that builds its workforce deliberately is in a stronger position than one that is always hiring in a hurry.
