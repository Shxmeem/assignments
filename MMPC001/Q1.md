# Management Approaches in Digital Organisations and an Integrated Framework for Hybrid and AI-Enabled Workplaces

## Introduction

Management theory has grown out of the problems of each era: the classical school tackled industrial efficiency, the behavioural school motivation, the systems school complexity, and the contingency school fit with the environment. Today, firms run on cloud platforms, rely on algorithms and AI for decisions, and treat hybrid work as permanent. This assignment asks whether theories from 1900 to 1970 still hold up. It argues that none is obsolete and none is enough alone. It evaluates each approach against digital work, then proposes an integrated framework for hybrid and AI-enabled workplaces.

**Classical approach.** Taylor's idea of breaking work into measured tasks lives on in warehouse software, call-centre analytics and productivity dashboards. Fayol's functions still describe managers' work, and Weber's rules appear in automated workflows and audit trails. In regulated sectors, formal procedure is required, and AI systems need defined decision rights and controls.

**Classical approach: limits**
The classical model treats workers as people who respond to pay and supervision, and it assumes stable, repeatable tasks. Knowledge work is ambiguous and creative, so it resists standardising. Algorithmic management can bring back Taylorism's worst traits, such as surveillance, lost autonomy and stress, while looking objective. It works as a control layer, not as a view of people.

**Behavioural approach**
This school (Hawthorne, Maslow, Herzberg, McGregor) focuses on motivation, groups and leadership. It matters in hybrid work, where belonging and trust no longer come from shared space. Psychological safety helps people speak up, and in remote teams silence is easily mistaken for agreement. Herzberg fits digital work: good tools and fair pay prevent dissatisfaction, while autonomy and purpose drive engagement. It also explains fear of AI and distrust of algorithmic decisions. Its weaknesses: happy staff are not always productive, structure and market pressure are ignored, it is hard to apply at scale, and most studies were of co-located workers.

**Systems approach**
Systems theory sees an organisation as connected parts that take inputs, produce outputs and adjust through feedback. Digital firms fit this well: platforms, data pipelines, suppliers and regulators depend on each other, and analytics and A/B tests create feedback. It helps managers spot side effects, like an AI tool that speeds up one team and overloads the next. But it is abstract, understates power and conflict, and needs tools like system maps and KPIs.

**Contingency approach**
This approach says the best way to manage depends on technology, environment, size and strategy. It is the most practical today because digital teams differ so much: a security team, a design studio and a support centre need different levels of structure. It also guides AI use: routine decisions can be automated, while sensitive ones need people. Its weakness is that it often just says "it depends", needs constant diagnosis and has no values of its own.

**Summary judgement**
The four approaches work best together. Classical gives structure, behavioural gives trust, systems gives awareness of links and learning, and contingency gives judgement. Used alone, each fails, which points to an integrated framework.

**Integrated framework (AHAMF)**
The framework has four layers, one per approach.

1. **Structure (classical):** clear roles, decision rights and accountability. It states which decisions AI makes, which it only recommends and which stay human, with a named owner for each automated process. Performance is judged by outcomes, not hours or keystrokes.
2. **People (behavioural):** psychological safety, autonomy, and inclusion so remote staff are not sidelined. Staff help choose AI tools, get reskilling and hear honestly about role changes.
3. **Systems (systems):** map dependencies before making changes, and run feedback loops such as retrospectives and AI reviews for accuracy, bias and drift. Regulators and customers are part of the picture.
4. **Calibration (contingency):** judge task uncertainty, risk and team maturity. Invoice processing suits heavy automation, creative teams need autonomy with AI as an assistant, and credit or triage decisions need human oversight. Collaborative work goes in person, focused work remote.
. Work modality is calibrated the same way: tasks needing intensive collaboration or relationship building are scheduled for in-person time, while focused individual work is suited to remote settings. Calibration is revisited regularly as conditions change.

### 3.5 Governing Principles

Four principles bind the layers together:

1. **Human accountability:** AI supports decisions but responsibility remains with identifiable people.
2. **Transparency and fairness:** employees and customers can understand how algorithmic decisions affecting them are made, and bias is audited.
3. **Outcome orientation:** performance is judged on results and contribution, not presence or surveillance.
4. **Continuous adaptation:** the framework itself is reviewed and revised as technology, regulation and work patterns evolve.

### 3.6 Implementation and Limitations

Implementation can proceed in stages: diagnosing current work and risk, setting structural and ethical baselines, piloting with selected teams, collecting feedback, then scaling. Success indicators could include employee engagement and retention, delivery quality and speed, AI error and bias rates, and equity of opportunity between remote and office-based staff. The framework has limitations. It demands managerial skill in diagnosis and judgement, may be costly for small firms, and has not been empirically tested here; it should be treated as a conceptual model to be validated through case studies or pilot implementation. Its effectiveness will also depend on organisational culture and on the quality of leadership.

## 4. Conclusion

This assignment evaluated four major approaches to management and examined their usefulness in contemporary digital organisations. The analysis found that each remains relevant but partial. Classical management provides the structure, accountability and measurable standards on which automated and regulated work depends, but its view of workers as passive and tasks as stable is unsuited to knowledge work, and its modern algorithmic form risks excessive surveillance. The behavioural approach explains why trust, autonomy and belonging matter in dispersed teams and during technological change, but it requires structure and performance discipline to be effective. The systems approach captures the interdependence of platforms, data, people and environments, and encourages feedback and learning, though it needs practical tools to move beyond abstraction. The contingency approach legitimately warns against universal solutions and provides logic for matching management style to circumstances, but it needs a value base to guide its choices.

Because these strengths and weaknesses offset one another, an integrated approach is more appropriate than selecting a single theory. The proposed Adaptive Human–AI Management Framework combines a classical structural foundation, a behavioural focus on people and culture, systems-based integration and learning, and contingency-based calibration, all bound by principles of human accountability, transparency, outcome orientation and continuous adaptation. It responds directly to the two defining features of the contemporary workplace: distributed, hybrid work, which makes human connection a managerial task, and AI, which makes the allocation of decisions between people and machines a central design question.

The central lesson is that technology changes the tools of management but not its fundamental problems of coordination, motivation, adaptation and fit. Managers who understand the enduring insights of all four traditions, and who apply them with judgement, will be best placed to lead organisations that are productive, fair and resilient. Future research could test the framework empirically across sectors and organisation sizes and examine how it evolves as AI capabilities advance.
