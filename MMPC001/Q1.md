# Management Approaches in Digital Organisations and an Integrated Framework for Hybrid and AI-Enabled Workplaces

*Assignment: Management Theory and Practice*

## 1. Introduction

Management theory has developed in response to the organisational problems of each era. The classical school answered the problem of industrial efficiency, the behavioural school the problem of human motivation, the systems school the problem of organisational complexity, and the contingency school the problem of fit between organisations and their environments. The twenty-first century presents a new set of conditions. Digital organisations operate on cloud platforms, rely on data and algorithms for decisions, and increasingly depend on artificial intelligence (AI) to perform tasks once reserved for human judgement. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, hybrid work, in which employees split time between office and remote locations, has become a lasting feature rather than an emergency arrangement.

These conditions raise a practical question: are theories developed between 1900 and 1970 still useful, or have they been made obsolete by technology? This assignment argues that none of the four approaches is obsolete, but none is sufficient alone. Each captures a real dimension of management that digital work has altered rather than removed. Classical thinking survives in algorithmic task design and performance measurement, but its assumptions about workers are inadequate. Behavioural theory explains engagement and psychological safety in dispersed teams, but it can be naive about technological and structural constraints. Systems thinking is arguably the most natural fit for platform-based, data-driven firms, though it is abstract and offers limited operational guidance. Contingency theory supplies the decision logic for choosing among approaches, but it provides no content of its own.

The assignment first evaluates each approach critically against the realities of digital organisations. It then proposes an integrated managerial framework, designed for hybrid and AI-enabled workplaces, that combines the strengths of all four while compensating for their weaknesses. The conclusion summarises the argument and notes the limits of the proposal.

## 2. Critical Evaluation of the Four Approaches

### 2.1 Classical Approach

The classical school comprises Taylor's scientific management, Fayol's administrative principles and Weber's bureaucracy. Its central ideas are task specialisation, measurable standards, clear hierarchy, unity of command and rule-based authority (Taylor, 1911; Fayol, 1916; Weber, 1947).

**Continuing relevance.** Taylor's logic of studying work, breaking it into tasks and measuring output has re-emerged in digital form. Warehouse management software, call-centre analytics, delivery-platform dispatching and productivity dashboards all decompose and monitor work at a granularity Taylor could not have imagined. Fayol's functions of planning, organising, commanding, coordinating and controlling remain a serviceable description of what managers do, and automated workflows, role definitions and audit trails reflect Weberian rule-based consistency. In regulated sectors such as banking, healthcare and data protection, formal procedure and clear accountability are not optional. AI systems also demand classical virtues: well-specified processes, defined decision rights and documented controls.

**Limitations.** The classical model treats workers as economic actors who respond to pay and supervision, and it assumes stable, repeatable tasks. Knowledge work in digital firms is neither. Software development, design, analysis and strategy involve ambiguity and creativity that resist standardisation. Algorithmic management can reproduce Taylorism's worst features, including intensive surveillance, loss of autonomy and stress, while appearing objective. Rigid hierarchy also slows the rapid iteration that agile and product-led organisations depend on. The classical approach is therefore relevant as a control and coordination layer but unreliable as a philosophy of people.

### 2.2 Behavioural Approach

The behavioural school grew from the Hawthorne studies (Mayo, 1933) and developed through the human relations movement and motivation theory, including Maslow's hierarchy of needs, Herzberg's two-factor theory and McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y. It emphasises motivation, group dynamics, leadership and the social nature of work.

**Continuing relevance.** This approach is highly relevant to hybrid work. When colleagues are physically dispersed, belonging, trust and recognition no longer arise naturally from shared space and must be deliberately managed. Evidence on psychological safety (Edmondson, 2019) shows that teams perform better when members can raise problems without fear, which matters especially in remote settings where silence is easily mistaken for agreement. Herzberg's distinction between hygiene factors and motivators maps onto digital work: reliable tools and fair pay prevent dissatisfaction, but autonomy, mastery and purpose drive engagement. Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan, 2000) extends this line and supports the autonomy-oriented design of remote work. Behavioural insight is also essential to AI adoption, since employee fear of displacement, distrust of algorithmic decisions and resistance to change are human responses that technology alone cannot resolve.

**Limitations.** The behavioural school has been criticised for assuming that satisfied employees are productive, an assumption the evidence only partly supports, and for neglecting structure, technology and market pressures. Its prescriptions can be hard to operationalise at scale, and an excessive focus on wellbeing without performance accountability can leave managers unable to address underperformance. Many findings were also derived from studies of co-located industrial workers and may transfer imperfectly to distributed, digitally mediated teams.

### 2.3 Systems Approach

Systems theory, drawing on von Bertalanffy (1968) and later Katz and Kahn (1978), views an organisation as an open system of interdependent subsystems that takes inputs from its environment, transforms them and returns outputs, adapting through feedback. Senge's (1990) concept of the learning organisation applies this thinking to management.

**Continuing relevance.** Digital organisations are visibly systems. Their performance depends on interdependence among data pipelines, software platforms, customer interfaces, suppliers and regulators, and a change in one component, such as a modified recommendation algorithm, propagates through the whole. Feedback loops are built directly into digital operations through analytics, A/B testing and continuous deployment. Platform ecosystems, in which firms depend on external developers, partners and users, make the open-system view especially apt. Systems thinking also helps managers anticipate unintended consequences: an AI tool that speeds up one process may overload a downstream team, and a hybrid policy designed for individual flexibility may fragment collaboration.

**Limitations.** Systems theory is highly abstract, and it is easier to describe a system than to manage one. It can understate the role of power, conflict and individual agency, and critics argue that it gives managers a vocabulary rather than concrete tools. In practice, it requires supporting methods such as systems mapping, scenario analysis and key performance indicators to become actionable. It also shares with the classical approach a risk of treating the organisation as a machine to be tuned, which can overlook ethical questions about whom the system serves.

### 2.4 Contingency Approach

The contingency approach holds that there is no universally best way to manage; effectiveness depends on the fit between organisational design and situational factors such as technology, environment, size and strategy. Key contributions include Burns and Stalker's (1961) distinction between mechanistic and organic structures, Woodward's (1965) work on technology and structure, Lawrence and Lorsch's (1967) differentiation and integration, and Fiedler's (1967) contingency model of leadership.

**Continuing relevance.** Contingency thinking is perhaps the most practically useful approach today, because digital organisations are highly heterogeneous. A cybersecurity operations team, a creative design studio and a customer-support centre face different task uncertainty and therefore need different degrees of structure and autonomy. Burns and Stalker's finding that organic structures suit unstable environments aligns with the flexible, cross-functional teams of technology firms, while stable, high-risk processes still justify mechanistic control. Contingency logic also guides AI deployment: routine, high-volume decisions suit automation, whereas ambiguous, ethically sensitive decisions require human judgement.

**Limitations.** Contingency theory is criticised for being descriptive rather than prescriptive: it says "it depends" without always specifying on what, or how to respond. It can produce complexity, as managers must continually diagnose situations and adjust, and the number of relevant variables in a fast-changing digital environment can overwhelm simple fit models. It also lacks a normative core, so it cannot by itself say what values should guide choices.

### 2.5 Summary Judgement

Taken together, the four approaches are complementary rather than competing. The classical school contributes **structure and control**, the behavioural school **motivation and trust**, the systems school **interdependence and learning**, and the contingency school **situational judgement**. Each fails when applied alone to digital work: classical management without behavioural insight breeds surveillance, behavioural management without structure lacks accountability, systems thinking without operational tools stays abstract, and contingency reasoning without principles becomes opportunism. This complementarity motivates an integrated framework.

## 3. An Integrated Managerial Framework for Hybrid and AI-Enabled Workplaces

The proposed framework, called here the **Adaptive Human–AI Management Framework (AHAMF)**, is organised in four layers, each drawing on one of the approaches, and governed by a set of guiding principles.

### 3.1 Layer 1: Structural Foundation (Classical)

This layer provides the minimum necessary structure. It includes clear role definitions, decision rights and escalation paths, documented processes, and defined accountability for outcomes. For AI-enabled work, it specifies which decisions AI may make autonomously, which it may recommend, and which are reserved for humans, with named human owners for every automated process. It also sets hybrid-work norms such as core collaboration hours, meeting protocols and equipment and security standards. The classical emphasis on measurable standards is retained but redirected from monitoring activity to measuring outcomes, so that results rather than hours or keystrokes define performance.

### 3.2 Layer 2: People and Culture (Behavioural)

This layer ensures that structure does not erode motivation. Its elements are:

- **Psychological safety and trust:** managers encourage open dissent, treat errors as learning opportunities and make participation easy for remote members.
- **Autonomy and purpose:** teams control how, and where possible when, they work, in line with self-determination theory.
- **Inclusion in hybrid settings:** deliberate practices prevent a two-tier workforce in which office-based staff gain visibility and promotion advantages over remote colleagues.
- **Human-centred AI adoption:** employees are involved in selecting and designing AI tools, receive reskilling opportunities and are given honest communication about role change.
- **Wellbeing:** boundaries against always-on availability and monitoring of workload and burnout.

### 3.3 Layer 3: Systems Integration and Learning (Systems)

This layer treats the organisation as an interconnected socio-technical system. Managers map dependencies among teams, data flows and AI tools before introducing changes, and assess likely downstream effects. Feedback loops operate at several levels: operational dashboards, regular team retrospectives, and periodic reviews of AI performance for accuracy, bias and drift. Knowledge is shared deliberately through documentation, communities of practice and cross-functional forums, supporting organisational learning (Senge, 1990). Importantly, this layer includes the external environment: regulators, customers, partners and society, whose expectations about data protection, transparency and fairness shape what AI use is acceptable.

### 3.4 Layer 4: Contingent Calibration (Contingency)

This layer determines how much of each of the other layers to apply in a given context. Managers diagnose three variables: **task uncertainty** (routine versus novel), **risk and regulatory exposure**, and **team maturity**. For example, a high-volume, low-uncertainty process such as invoice processing warrants heavy automation and tight structure; a creative or strategic team warrants high autonomy, minimal procedure and AI used as an assistant rather than a decider; a high-risk domain such as credit decisions or medical triage warrants human oversight, auditability and strict controls regardless of efficiency gains. Work modality is calibrated the same way: tasks needing intensive collaboration or relationship building are scheduled for in-person time, while focused individual work is suited to remote settings. Calibration is revisited regularly as conditions change.

### 3.5 Governing Principles

Four principles bind the layers together:

1. **Human accountability:** AI supports decisions but responsibility remains with identifiable people.
2. **Transparency and fairness:** employees and customers can understand how algorithmic decisions affecting them are made, and bias is audited.
3. **Outcome orientation:** performance is judged on results and contribution, not presence or surveillance.
4. **Continuous adaptation:** the framework itself is reviewed and revised as technology, regulation and work patterns evolve.

### 3.6 Implementation and Limitations

Implementation can proceed in stages: diagnosing current work and risk, setting structural and ethical baselines, piloting with selected teams, collecting feedback, then scaling. Success indicators could include employee engagement and retention, delivery quality and speed, AI error and bias rates, and equity of opportunity between remote and office-based staff. The framework has limitations. It demands managerial skill in diagnosis and judgement, may be costly for small firms, and has not been empirically tested here; it should be treated as a conceptual model to be validated through case studies or pilot implementation. Its effectiveness will also depend on organisational culture and on the quality of leadership.

## 4. Conclusion

This assignment evaluated four major approaches to management and examined their usefulness in contemporary digital organisations. The analysis found that each remains relevant but partial. Classical management provides the structure, accountability and measurable standards on which automated and regulated work depends, but its view of workers as passive and tasks as stable is unsuited to knowledge work, and its modern algorithmic form risks excessive surveillance. The behavioural approach explains why trust, autonomy and belonging matter in dispersed teams and during technological change, but it requires structure and performance discipline to be effective. The systems approach captures the interdependence of platforms, data, people and environments, and encourages feedback and learning, though it needs practical tools to move beyond abstraction. The contingency approach legitimately warns against universal solutions and provides logic for matching management style to circumstances, but it needs a value base to guide its choices.

Because these strengths and weaknesses offset one another, an integrated approach is more appropriate than selecting a single theory. The proposed Adaptive Human–AI Management Framework combines a classical structural foundation, a behavioural focus on people and culture, systems-based integration and learning, and contingency-based calibration, all bound by principles of human accountability, transparency, outcome orientation and continuous adaptation. It responds directly to the two defining features of the contemporary workplace: distributed, hybrid work, which makes human connection a managerial task, and AI, which makes the allocation of decisions between people and machines a central design question.

The central lesson is that technology changes the tools of management but not its fundamental problems of coordination, motivation, adaptation and fit. Managers who understand the enduring insights of all four traditions, and who apply them with judgement, will be best placed to lead organisations that are productive, fair and resilient. Future research could test the framework empirically across sectors and organisation sizes and examine how it evolves as AI capabilities advance.
