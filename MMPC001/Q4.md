# Organisational Design for Innovation and Sustainability in a Growing Start-up

## Introduction

A start-up usually begins with a handful of people sitting close together. Everyone knows what everyone else is doing, decisions are made in a conversation, and the culture is simply the habits of the founders. This is why small firms often innovate faster than large ones. The trouble starts when the firm grows. Teams are added, offices open in other countries, and the founders can no longer talk to everyone. Many start-ups lose their speed at this stage, and some fall apart, because the informal ways that worked for 20 people do not work for 2,000.

Three things decide whether a growing firm keeps its ability to create new things and survive over the long term: how the organisation is structured, how information moves through it, and what people believe is normal behaviour. This assignment first evaluates how these three work together. It then proposes an organisational design for a start-up becoming a global company and explains how that design deals with communication barriers, cultural integration and the need to change direction when conditions change.

## How Structure, Communication and Culture Work Together

**Structure.** Structure decides who has authority and who reports to whom. Burns and Stalker described two extremes. A rigid structure has clear ranks, fixed roles and decisions made at the top. It is efficient when work is predictable. A flexible structure has loose roles, shared decisions and quick changes. It suits uncertain work. Innovation needs the second type, but sustainability needs some of the first. A company with no rules wastes money, repeats work and makes costly mistakes. A company with too many rules stops producing new ideas. The challenge is finding the right balance, and the right balance changes as the firm grows.

**Communication.** Communication decides whether the right information reaches the right person in time. In a small firm this happens by talking across the room. In a large firm it needs a system. When communication is poor, people in different departments solve the same problem twice, bad news is hidden until it is too late, and good ideas from junior staff never reach anyone who can act on them. Innovation depends heavily on information moving sideways between departments, not only up and down. Sustainability depends on early warning, because small problems that are reported late become large ones.

**Culture.** Schein explained culture as having layers: what can be seen (offices, rules, titles), what the company says it values, and the unspoken beliefs that actually guide behaviour. The third layer matters most. A company can say it welcomes new ideas, but if the last person who tried something and failed was quietly pushed aside, employees learn that safe behaviour is rewarded. Culture decides whether people speak up, share what they know, and take reasonable risks.

**How they affect each other.** The three cannot be judged separately. A flat structure with a fear-based culture produces silence, so no ideas come out. A good culture with a slow, layered structure produces frustrated people whose ideas die waiting for approval. A fast structure with poor communication produces teams that move quickly in different directions. For example, a firm may set up small independent teams to encourage innovation, but if those teams do not share what they learn, the firm pays for the same lessons many times. Innovation needs all three working in the same direction. Sustainability needs the same, with the addition that the firm must be able to adjust all three as conditions change. The most common cause of failure during rapid growth is that one of the three changes (usually structure, because new people are added) while the other two stay the same.

## Proposed Design: The Shared Core, Local Teams Model

The idea behind this design is simple. Keep a small centre that holds the things which must be the same everywhere, and give most decision power to teams that are close to customers and the actual work. The model has three parts, matching the three factors above.

**1. Structure.**

- *A small global core.* This holds only what must be consistent: the main technology platform, financial controls, legal compliance, brand standards and the company's core values. It does not make daily business decisions for regions.
- *Regional units with real authority.* Each major region (for example, Asia, Europe, the Americas) has its own head, who has authority over hiring, local pricing, partnerships and product changes for that market, within agreed limits. A clear table of decision rights states which decisions belong to the core, which to the region, and which to the team. This avoids the common problem of people not knowing who can say yes.
- *Small mixed teams.* Within each region, work is done by teams of about 6 to 10 people who bring together the skills needed to finish a piece of work (engineering, design, sales, support), so that the work does not need to be passed between departments.
- *Limited layers.* The company keeps the number of management levels as low as possible, so that no more than four or five levels separate a new employee from the top.

**2. Communication.**

- *Writing first.* In a global company, people work in different time zones. Important decisions, plans and reasons are written in a shared, searchable place, so that someone in another country can read them later and does not depend on having been in a meeting.
- *A decision record.* Each major decision is recorded with who decided, why, and what other options were considered. This helps new employees understand past choices and reduces repeated arguments.
- *Planned live contact.* Teams in different time zones have a fixed, small number of overlap hours. Meeting times rotate so that the same region does not always have to join late at night.
- *Plain language.* Since English may be a second language for many staff, the company encourages short, simple writing and avoids slang and idioms. Important documents are checked for clarity.
- *Upward and sideways routes.* Leaders hold regular open sessions where any employee can ask questions, and each region sends representatives to a regular cross-region meeting to share what has worked and what has failed.

**3. Culture.**

- *Few core values, described as behaviours.* Instead of long lists of words, the company chooses three or four values and states what each looks like in daily work. For example, "we share bad news early" is a behaviour and can be seen, while "integrity" alone is not.
- *Local adaptation.* The core values remain the same, but each region may adjust how they are practised. For example, a direct style of giving feedback may need to be softened in a culture where openly disagreeing with a senior person is considered rude.
- *Mixing people.* Employees spend time in other regions through short exchanges. Leadership teams include people from several countries. Hiring includes screening for how a person works with others, not only technical skill.
- *Onboarding.* Each new employee has a buddy from another team or region for the first few months, which builds relationships across borders from the start.
- *Handling failure.* Leaders openly discuss their own mistakes and what was learned. Teams review failed projects to understand causes, and not to find someone to blame.

## How the Design Addresses the Three Problems

**Communication barriers.** The main barriers in a global firm are distance, time zones, language and the tendency for each region to keep information to itself. The written-first approach and the decision record reduce dependence on being in the same place at the same time. The plain-language rule reduces misunderstanding. The cross-region meetings and the rotating schedule make information move sideways, which is where innovation usually needs it. Because decision rights are written down, confusion about who is responsible is also reduced, which is another common source of delay.

**Cultural integration.** A global firm often grows by hiring in new countries or buying other companies, and the people who join bring their own habits. If the founders' culture is forced on everyone, newcomers feel unwelcome and the best of them leave. If every region does its own thing, the company becomes several separate companies. The model avoids both by holding a small set of shared values and letting regions decide how to practise them. Exchanges, mixed leadership and buddy systems build real relationships, which are what actually hold a culture together across distance. Describing values as visible behaviours also makes it possible to check whether people follow them.

**Strategic flexibility.** A company that has grown large can no longer change direction as easily as a small one. The design protects flexibility in four ways. First, regional units can respond to local changes without waiting for headquarters. Second, the small teams can be formed, moved or closed without redesigning the whole company. Third, a small part of each region's budget is set aside for trying new ideas, with the understanding that some will fail. Fourth, the company reviews its own structure once a year and asks whether decision rights, team sizes and reporting lines still match the work. Projects and products that no longer serve a purpose are closed on a regular schedule, so that old work does not use up the people and money needed for new work.

**Possible weaknesses.** The design has costs. Giving regions authority risks inconsistent quality, which is why the core must hold firm standards in areas such as security, finance and law. Written communication takes time and discipline, and may feel slow to people used to quick conversations. Running a decision-rights table and yearly reviews requires effort from managers. The model also depends on leaders being willing to give up some control, which is hard for founders who built the company themselves. These are manageable, but they should be planned for.

## Conclusion

Structure, communication and culture cannot be treated as separate matters. Structure decides who can act, communication decides what they know when they act, and culture decides whether they are willing to act and speak honestly. Innovation and long-term survival depend on all three pointing in the same direction. Rapid growth tends to break this balance because one factor changes faster than the others, and the firm slows down or loses its identity.

The Shared Core, Local Teams model tries to keep the balance. A small centre keeps the firm consistent, regional units and small teams keep it quick, written and planned communication keeps people informed across distance, and a few clearly described values keep people connected to the same purpose. The design is not perfect and needs strong standards at the centre, discipline in communication and leaders who are comfortable sharing authority. But a start-up that grows without deliberately designing these three things usually finds that they have been designed for it by accident, and not in a way that helps.
