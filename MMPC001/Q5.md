# Transforming a Manufacturing Organisation: Change, Ethics and Responsibility in India

## Introduction

Many manufacturing companies are now installing sensors, automated machines and data systems to cut waste and raise output. This is usually presented as a purely technical project. But a factory is also a place where people work, where land, water and air are used, and where the company's honesty is tested every day. When a firm modernises its machines while workers complain about long hours, low pay or unsafe conditions, and neighbours complain about pollution, the digital project is not enough. The public sees a company that has upgraded its technology but not its behaviour.

This assignment looks at an organisation in that position. It proposes a strategy that treats digital change, ethical leadership and social responsibility as one plan rather than three separate programmes. It then assesses the difficulties of carrying out such a plan in India, where the opportunities are large but the conditions are demanding.

## The Core Problem

The criticism the company faces is not a side issue. It affects whether the digital transformation will succeed. If workers believe that new machines are only a way to cut jobs, they will resist them, work around them or feed wrong data into the system. If the company is known for poor labour practices, good engineers will not join it, and large customers and lenders, many of whom now check suppliers' records, may look elsewhere. If the leadership is seen as dishonest, no one will believe its promises about change. So the three problems share one root: people do not trust the company. A strategy that ignores this will fix the machines and lose the people.

## The Proposed Strategy: Fix, Involve, Report

The strategy has three parts, and the order matters. The company first corrects what is visibly wrong, then brings people into the change, and then reports its progress honestly.

**1. Fix what is wrong (ethical leadership).** Change cannot begin with speeches. It begins with the leaders being willing to look at the problems openly.

- The top team orders an independent review of labour conditions, safety, wages, overtime, contract workers and environmental impact. The findings are shared with employees and not kept inside the boardroom.
- Leaders personally take responsibility. The managing director and plant heads visit the shop floor regularly, listen to workers directly and answer questions without a script.
- Simple, enforceable rules are written down: wages paid fully and on time, overtime recorded and paid, no unsafe work, no pressure to hide accidents. Ethical behaviour is built into performance reviews, so a manager who meets production targets by breaking these rules is not rewarded.
- A safe way to complain is set up, such as a helpline or a committee with worker representatives, and the company shows that complaints lead to action and that no one is punished for raising them.

Ethical leadership here means that the leader's own conduct matches the rules. Employees watch what leaders do when targets are at risk, and that decides whether the rules are believed.

**2. Involve people in the change (change management).** Lewin described change as unfreezing old habits, moving to new ones and then fixing them in place. Kotter added that people need to understand why the change is urgent and see early results. The strategy follows these ideas in plain steps.

- *Explain honestly.* Workers are told what the new technology will do, which jobs will change and which may disappear. Hiding this creates rumours and fear.
- *Promise and keep a commitment on jobs.* Where possible, the company commits to retraining before any layoffs, and tells workers exactly which new skills will be taught. Operators can learn to run, maintain or check automated machines, and the company pays for this training.
- *Start small.* One plant or one production line is used as a trial. Workers on that line help choose how the new system is set up, because they know the practical problems. Results, including mistakes, are shared before the change spreads to other plants.
- *Use respected people.* Supervisors and senior workers who are trusted by their colleagues are trained first and help teach others. This works better than instructions from distant managers.
- *Check regularly.* Short, regular surveys and meetings measure whether people understand the changes and where the problems are. Plans are adjusted based on what is heard.

**3. Report honestly and act beyond the factory (corporate social responsibility).** Social responsibility should be part of how the business runs, not a separate donation budget.

- *Environment.* The digital systems that track machines can also track energy, water use, waste and emissions. The company sets clear reduction targets, such as using less energy per unit produced, and publishes the results. Sensors that help reduce waste save money, so this part pays for itself over time.
- *Workers.* The company extends fair treatment to contract workers and suppliers' employees, not only permanent staff. Supplier agreements include labour and safety conditions, and suppliers who repeatedly break them are dropped after warnings.
- *Community.* The legally required CSR spending is directed towards needs identified with local people, such as skill training, health care and clean water near the plants, and is followed up to see whether anything actually improved.
- *Governance.* A board member is made responsible for ethics and sustainability, an independent person checks the published numbers, and the company reports on its progress each year, including targets it missed.

**How the three parts connect.** Fixing problems gives the company the credibility to ask people to accept change. Involving people makes the technology actually work and protects jobs. Honest reporting keeps the company from slipping back once attention fades. Digital systems support all three, as they give real numbers on safety, energy and working hours, which are harder to hide than verbal claims.

## Challenges of Implementation in India

The strategy sounds reasonable, but India presents difficulties that need to be assessed honestly.

**Large informal and contract workforce.** A large share of Indian manufacturing labour works through contractors, on short agreements and without formal protections. Companies often reduce their direct responsibility by hiring this way. Extending fair treatment to these workers raises costs and requires monitoring people the company does not directly employ. Contractors may also resist, since stricter rules reduce their margins.

**Weak enforcement and mixed incentives.** India has laws on factory safety, wages, pollution and CSR, and the labour laws have recently been reorganised into new codes. But enforcement varies between states and inspectors are often limited in number. A company that follows the rules can be undercut by competitors that do not, which makes ethical behaviour feel like a competitive disadvantage. Some firms respond by doing the minimum, such as meeting the CSR spending requirement as a formality without checking results.

**Price pressure and thin margins.** Many Indian manufacturers, especially small and medium firms that supply larger companies, work on low margins and are pushed by buyers to cut prices. Investments in safety, cleaner processes and fair wages are hard to afford, and the benefits come slowly. Large global buyers say they care about ethics but still often choose the lowest bidder, which weakens the case for doing the right thing.

**Skills gap and fear of job loss.** Retraining is easy to promise and hard to deliver. Many workers have limited formal education, and large numbers of them need new skills quickly. In a country with many people looking for jobs, workers also know they are easily replaced, so they fear that automation will cost them their income. Trade unions in some regions may resist change because of past experiences of broken promises, and management may find it hard to build trust quickly.

**Cultural habits in management.** Many Indian organisations have strong hierarchies in which seniors decide and juniors do not question them, and family-run firms may keep decisions within the family. This makes honest upward communication difficult. Workers may not report problems out of fear, and managers may not pass on bad news. The complaint system and open leadership visits in the strategy depend on changing this habit, which takes years, not months.

**Infrastructure and regional differences.** Unreliable electricity, uneven internet access and differences between states mean that a digital system working in one plant may not work in another. Environmental targets are also harder where the energy supply depends heavily on coal.

**Short-term thinking.** Quarterly results, pressure from investors and frequent changes in leadership encourage short-term decisions. The strategy requires several years of consistent effort, and a new leader or a bad quarter can easily end it.

These challenges do not make the strategy unworkable, but they show that it cannot be imposed from the top or completed quickly. The company should begin where it has the most control (its own plants and direct employees), prove that it works, and then extend it to suppliers. It should also be realistic about costs, because some of the benefits, such as lower staff turnover, better safety records and stronger customer confidence, appear only after a few years.

## Conclusion

A manufacturing company that modernises its technology while ignoring complaints about labour, the environment and governance is building on weak ground, because the people who must run the new systems do not trust it. The strategy proposed here, fixing what is wrong, involving people in the change and reporting honestly, treats these problems as connected. Ethical leadership gives the change credibility, change management helps people accept it, and social responsibility keeps the company accountable to workers, neighbours and society.

In India, the plan faces real obstacles: a large contract workforce, uneven enforcement, price pressure, skill shortages, hierarchical habits and short-term thinking. These make progress slower and more expensive than in some other countries. But they also show why the effort matters, since companies that treat workers and the environment fairly are better placed to keep skilled staff, win long-term customers and avoid costly scandals. The company's real test is whether its leaders keep to the plan when it becomes inconvenient, because trust is built through that consistency and not through announcements.
