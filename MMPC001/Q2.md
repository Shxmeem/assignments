# Restoring Organisational Effectiveness in a Multinational Organisation: A Managerial Action Plan

*Assignment: Management Consultancy Report on Planning, Organising, Staffing and Directing*

## 1. Introduction

Multinational organisations gain scale, market access and talent diversity, but they also inherit problems of distance, culture and complexity. The organisation in this case shows three connected symptoms: declining employee productivity, role ambiguity, and coordination failures across geographically dispersed teams. These are rarely isolated. When roles are unclear, employees duplicate or neglect tasks; when coordination between locations fails, work is delayed and reworked; and when effort does not visibly translate into results, motivation and productivity fall. Together they signal weaknesses in the basic machinery of management rather than a shortage of effort or talent.

This report adopts the position of an external management consultant. It uses the classical functional framework first set out by Fayol (1916) and refined by Koontz and O'Donnell, which divides managerial work into planning, organising, staffing and directing (with controlling closing the loop). This framework is appropriate because the symptoms map directly onto it: unclear objectives and priorities point to planning, ambiguous roles and weak coordination to organising, capability and fit problems to staffing, and falling motivation and poor communication to directing. Each function is supported by established theory, including Drucker's management by objectives, Locke's goal-setting theory, Mintzberg's coordination mechanisms, Hackman and Oldham's job characteristics model, Herzberg's two-factor theory, Vroom's expectancy theory, Hofstede's cultural dimensions and situational leadership.

The report first sets out a short diagnosis, then presents an action plan for each of the four functions, justified by theory and practice, together with an implementation and control approach. The conclusion summarises the plan, expected outcomes and its limitations. Because the facts of the case are limited, certain assumptions are made explicit: that the organisation operates across several time zones and cultures, that it has regional units reporting to a global headquarters, and that no single cause explains the decline in productivity.

## 2. Diagnosis and Managerial Action Plan

### 2.1 Diagnosis of the Problem

The three symptoms point to a likely set of root causes. First, **strategic cascade failure**: global objectives are probably not translated into clear regional and individual goals, so teams optimise locally. Second, **structural ambiguity**: overlapping matrix reporting lines, informal role boundaries and unclear decision rights, a well-known issue in multinationals (Galbraith, 2009). Third, **weak coordination mechanisms**: dispersed teams rely on ad hoc email and meetings rather than designed processes, while differences in time zone, language and culture add friction (Hofstede, 2001). Fourth, **motivational and leadership gaps**: employees lack clarity, feedback and recognition. A consultant would confirm these through employee surveys, process mapping, interviews with regional managers and analysis of productivity and rework data before finalising the plan.

### 2.2 Planning: Establishing Direction and Clarity

Planning is the foundation, because unclear direction produces both role ambiguity and wasted effort. The recommended actions are:

- **Clarify strategy and cascade objectives.** Headquarters should restate a small number of strategic priorities and cascade them through a management by objectives (MBO) process (Drucker, 1954), so that each region, team and individual has goals aligned to the global strategy.
- **Set SMART, participative goals.** Locke and Latham's (1990) goal-setting theory shows that specific, challenging and accepted goals raise performance. Targets should be agreed with employees rather than imposed, which also improves commitment.
- **Adopt a balanced scorecard.** Kaplan and Norton's (1992) scorecard links financial results with customer, internal process and learning measures, preventing short-term productivity pressure from undermining capability.
- **Plan for contingencies and local fit.** Global frameworks should leave defined space for regional adaptation, reflecting contingency theory (Lawrence and Lorsch, 1967), and scenario planning should cover currency, regulatory and talent risks.
- **Create an integrated planning calendar.** A common global cycle for annual, quarterly and monthly planning, with agreed reporting dates across time zones, replaces uncoordinated local planning.

The justification is that productivity depends on employees knowing what matters and how success is measured. Practice at firms such as Google, where objectives and key results are openly shared, supports this approach.

### 2.3 Organising: Defining Roles and Designing Coordination

Organising addresses role ambiguity and coordination failure directly.

- **Conduct a role and structure audit.** Job descriptions should be rewritten around outcomes, with clear authority, responsibility and accountability, following Fayol's principles of unity of command and the scalar chain. Hackman and Oldham's (1976) job characteristics model suggests designing roles with task identity, significance and autonomy to improve motivation as well as clarity.
- **Clarify decision rights using a RACI matrix.** For each major process, the matrix specifies who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed, reducing duplication and decision delays, especially in matrix structures.
- **Choose a fit-for-purpose structure.** Where product and regional lines compete, the organisation should clarify a primary reporting line and a secondary, dotted-line relationship, or adopt global process owners for activities such as supply chain and customer service. Mintzberg (1979) argues that structure should match the coordinating mechanism needed; complex, non-routine work requires mutual adjustment and liaison roles rather than rigid standardisation.
- **Build designed coordination mechanisms.** These include global cross-functional teams, liaison officers, regional hubs, shared digital platforms for project and document management, standardised handover protocols and "follow-the-sun" arrangements in which work passes between time zones.
- **Balance centralisation and decentralisation.** Strategic, brand and compliance decisions remain central, while operational decisions move closer to the local market, speeding response and increasing ownership.

The theoretical justification combines classical clarity of authority with contingency logic and Mintzberg's coordination mechanisms. In practice, many multinationals have reduced confusion by appointing global process owners and publishing decision-rights charters.

### 2.4 Staffing: Right People, Right Roles, Right Capabilities

Even a sound structure fails if people lack fit or capability.

- **Workforce and skills planning.** A skills audit identifies gaps against strategic needs, and the results guide recruitment, redeployment and training.
- **Selection aligned to roles and culture.** Recruitment should use role-specific competencies, structured interviews and assessment of intercultural competence for international assignments. Perlmutter's (1969) ethnocentric, polycentric and geocentric staffing orientations suggest a geocentric approach, in which the best person is selected regardless of nationality, to build a global management cadre.
- **Training and development.** Programmes should cover role-specific skills, virtual collaboration, cross-cultural communication and leadership. Kirkpatrick's four-level model can evaluate whether training changes behaviour and results.
- **Fair performance appraisal.** Appraisal should link to MBO goals, use multiple raters such as 360-degree feedback, and be calibrated across regions so that cultural differences in rating do not create inequity.
- **Career paths, mobility and succession.** Clear progression and international rotation motivate staff, spread knowledge between units and reduce attrition.
- **Fair and competitive reward.** Pay systems should respect local market conditions while maintaining global fairness and rewarding contribution to shared goals.

This function draws on human resource management and on Herzberg's (1959) distinction between hygiene factors, such as pay and job security, and motivators such as growth and achievement; both must be addressed to sustain productivity.

### 2.5 Directing: Leading, Motivating and Communicating

Directing converts plans and structures into action.

- **Adaptive leadership.** Hersey and Blanchard's situational leadership suggests that managers should vary direction and support according to team maturity. Newer teams or those in transition need more guidance, while experienced teams need autonomy. Leaders should also be trained to manage remotely and across cultures.
- **Motivation through expectancy and equity.** Vroom's (1964) expectancy theory holds that motivation depends on the belief that effort leads to performance, performance to rewards, and rewards are valued. Managers should therefore provide clear goals, resources, transparent recognition and meaningful rewards. Equity theory (Adams, 1963) reminds them to keep rewards visibly fair across locations.
- **Communication discipline.** Daft and Lengel's media richness theory suggests using rich media such as video calls for ambiguous or sensitive matters and leaner channels for routine updates. A communication charter should set response norms, meeting rotation to share time-zone burden, and clear escalation routes.
- **Team building and trust.** Tuckman's stages of team development and Edmondson's (1999) research on psychological safety support structured onboarding, periodic in-person meetings, and open feedback to build trust in dispersed teams.
- **Cultural sensitivity.** Hofstede's dimensions, such as power distance and individualism, warn managers that styles of feedback, participation and decision-making will differ between regions and must be adapted.

### 2.6 Implementation, Monitoring and Control

The plan should be phased: a diagnostic and quick-win stage in the first three months (role clarification, communication charter, RACI for critical processes), a redesign stage over the following six months (structure, goal cascade, training), and a consolidation stage thereafter. A steering committee with regional representation should oversee progress. Key indicators include productivity per employee, rework and delay rates, role-clarity and engagement scores, cross-regional project cycle time, attrition and training transfer. Regular review closes the control loop and allows adjustment. Anticipated obstacles are resistance to change and local autonomy concerns, which Kotter's (1996) change model addresses through a clear case for change, visible leadership support, early wins and communication of progress.

## 3. Conclusion

The multinational's declining productivity, role ambiguity and coordination failures reflect weaknesses across all four basic managerial functions, and the plan therefore addresses them together rather than separately. Planning restores strategic clarity through cascaded, participative objectives and a balanced scorecard. Organising resolves ambiguity by redefining roles, assigning decision rights through a RACI matrix and building deliberate coordination mechanisms suited to dispersed work. Staffing ensures that capable people are selected, developed and rewarded fairly within a geocentric, skills-based approach. Directing sustains performance through adaptive leadership, motivation grounded in expectancy and equity, disciplined communication and culturally aware team building.

The recommendations are justified by a consistent body of theory. Fayol's functions provide the framework; Drucker and Locke explain why goal clarity raises performance; Mintzberg and contingency theory explain how structure and coordination should fit complex international work; Hackman and Oldham and Herzberg link job design and reward to motivation; and Vroom, Hofstede and situational leadership shape how people are led across cultures. Practice at global firms supports many of these measures, such as global process owners, shared objectives and structured virtual collaboration.

If implemented, the plan should shorten decision and delivery times, reduce duplicated effort, improve engagement and raise productivity within roughly 12 to 18 months, measurable through the indicators identified. It also has limitations. It rests on assumptions about the organisation's causes of decline that must be verified through detailed diagnosis; it requires sustained commitment from senior leadership and investment in training and technology; and cultural differences mean that no single design will work equally well in every location. Reviewing and adapting the plan regularly is therefore essential. Ultimately, restoring effectiveness depends less on any single technique than on aligning direction, structure, people and leadership so that they reinforce one another.
