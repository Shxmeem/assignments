# A Management Action Plan to Restore Effectiveness in a Multinational Organisation

*Assignment: Planning, Organising, Staffing and Directing*

## 1. Introduction

Large companies that work in many countries often reach a point where things start to slip. The organisation in this case has reached that point. Employees are producing less now, many actually doesn't know what part their job covers and teams in different regions failing to work together.. These three problems feed each other. When a person does not know what they are responsible for, they either do too little or repeat what someone else is already doing. When two offices do not coordinate, work gets delayed or done twice. When people see their effort wasted, they stop trying as hard, and productivity falls further.

In this report as a outside management consultant My point is that the troubles are not because of lack of talent or anything else. It is a weakness in the basic everyday tasks of management. I have therefore organised the plan around the four functions that Henri Fayol (1916) described and that later writers such as Koontz and O'Donnell developed
which are:
planning, organising, staffing and directing. Controlling, the fifth function, is used at the end to check whether the plan is working. The four functions match the problems well. Unclear priorities are a planning issue. Unclear roles and poor coordination are organising issues. Skill gaps and poor fit are staffing issues. Low motivation and poor communication are directing issues.

Each recommendation is supported by a well-known theory or by common practice in international firms. The report begins with a short diagnosis of the likely causes. It then sets out actions under each of the four functions, followed by a short note on how to put the plan into effect and how to measure it. The conclusion pulls the points together and admits the limits of the advice. Since the case gives few facts, I assume that the company has several regional offices in different time zones, reports to a head office, and has no single cause for its problems.

## 2. Diagnosis and Action Plan

### 2.1 What is probably going wrong

Before recommending anything, a consultant should find out what is really happening. I would start with staff surveys, interviews with regional managers and a look at figures on output, delays and rework. From the symptoms, four causes seem most likely. First, the head office's goals probably do not reach the people doing the work in a clear form, so each region sets its own priorities. Second, reporting lines are likely tangled, especially if the company uses a matrix structure where people answer to both a regional boss and a product boss (Galbraith, 2009). Third, coordination between regions depends on emails and occasional calls rather than agreed routines, and differences in time zone, language and culture make this harder (Hofstede, 2001). Fourth, many employees probably get little feedback or recognition, which weakens motivation.

### 2.2 Planning: deciding where to go

Planning comes first because roles and teamwork cannot be sorted out until people know what the company is trying to achieve. I recommend four steps.

The head office should restate its main aims in plain terms and pass them down through every level using management by objectives (Drucker, 1954). Each region, team and person then has goals that clearly link to the company's overall direction. These goals should be agreed with employees in discussion, not simply handed down. Locke and Latham (1990) found that clear, reasonably difficult goals that people accept lead to better performance than vague goals or goals people feel are forced on them.

Second, performance should be measured on more than profit. The balanced scorecard of Kaplan and Norton (1992) looks at finance, customers, internal processes and learning. This stops managers from pushing for short-term output at the cost of the skills the company needs later.

Third, the plan should leave room for local conditions. Lawrence and Lorsch (1967) showed that units facing different environments need different arrangements. A rule that works in Germany may not suit India or Brazil, so head office should fix the aims and the non-negotiable standards but let regions choose how to reach them.

Finally, all regions should follow one planning calendar, with common dates for yearly, quarterly and monthly reviews. Without this, regions plan on their own timetables and cannot see how their work fits together.

### 2.3 Organising: making roles and teamwork clear

This function deals directly with role confusion and poor coordination. The first job is a review of every major role. Each job description should say what the person is responsible for, what decisions they can make and who they report to. Fayol's idea of unity of command, that a person should have one main boss, applies here. Where a matrix structure is needed, one reporting line should be named as the main one and the other as a supporting line.

For important processes, I suggest a simple chart that lists who does the work, who is finally accountable, who must be asked for advice and who just needs to be told. This is often called a RACI chart. It removes the guesswork about who decides, which is where much of the delay in multinational firms comes from.

The design of the jobs themselves also matters. Hackman and Oldham (1976) argued that people work better when their job is a complete piece of work, when they can see that it matters and when they have some freedom in how they do it. Roles should be written with these points in mind, not only to define duties.

Coordination should be planned, not left to chance. Mintzberg (1979) described several ways in which organisations coordinate work, from written rules and standard procedures to direct contact between people. Complex international work cannot rely on rules alone. I recommend liaison people between regions, cross-regional project teams for shared work, and a common online space for plans and documents. Handing work from one time zone to the next at the end of each day, with a short written note, can also save time. Finally, decisions on brand, legal compliance and strategy should stay at the centre, while day-to-day decisions should move closer to customers.

### 2.4 Staffing: the right people in the right jobs

A good structure still fails if the people in it lack the skills or are in the wrong place. The company should begin with a skills check to see where gaps exist, and use the result to decide who to hire, who to move and who to train.

For hiring and promotion, Perlmutter (1969) described three approaches: favouring people from the home country, favouring local people in each country, or choosing the best person wherever they come from. The third, which he called geocentric, suits a firm that wants a truly global team and a pool of capable leaders. Selection should use clear job-related criteria and structured interviews, and for overseas posts it should check how well a person copes with different cultures.

Training should cover job skills, working with remote colleagues, cross-cultural communication and basic leadership. Kirkpatrick's four levels, which look at reaction, learning, behaviour and results, give a practical way to test whether training changed anything. Performance appraisal should tie back to the agreed goals, use feedback from several people, and be adjusted between regions so that cultural habits in rating, such as some countries scoring generously and others strictly, do not create unfairness.

Career paths and spells in other countries give staff a reason to stay and help knowledge move around the company. Pay should reflect local markets but remain fair when compared across regions. Herzberg (1959) is useful here: pay and job security prevent dissatisfaction, but only responsibility, achievement and growth really motivate. A company needs both.

### 2.5 Directing: leading and motivating people

Directing turns plans into daily action. Managers should adjust their style to the team in front of them. Under Hersey and Blanchard's situational leadership, new or struggling teams need clear instructions and close support, while experienced teams do better with more freedom. Managers of remote teams need training in this, since they cannot simply walk past a desk to check progress.

For motivation, Vroom's (1964) expectancy theory is practical. People work harder when they believe effort leads to good performance, good performance leads to a reward, and the reward matters to them. Managers can act on each link by giving clear goals and the right resources, by recognising results openly, and by offering rewards that people actually value. Adams's (1963) equity theory adds that people compare their treatment with others, so rewards need to look fair across offices.

Communication needs rules. Daft and Lengel's media richness theory says that complicated or sensitive matters should be handled through richer channels such as video calls, while simple updates can go by email. The company should write down basic norms: how quickly people should reply, how meeting times rotate so one region does not always work at night, and who to go to when something is stuck. Trust is built through occasional face-to-face meetings, a good start for new members and an atmosphere where people can raise problems without fear (Edmondson, 1999). Managers should also remember that attitudes to authority and feedback differ across cultures (Hofstede, 2001), so one style will not work everywhere.

### 2.6 Putting the plan into practice

I suggest three stages. In the first three months, deal with quick matters: clarify the most important roles, agree communication rules and draw up decision charts for the main processes. Over the next six months, redesign reporting lines, pass goals down, and begin training. After that, review and adjust. A steering group with members from each region should watch progress. Useful measures are output per employee, delays and rework, staff surveys on role clarity and engagement, time taken by cross-regional projects, and staff turnover. Resistance should be expected. Kotter (1996) advises explaining clearly why change is needed, getting visible support from senior people and showing early successes.

## 3. Conclusion

The problems in this multinational, falling productivity, unclear roles and poor coordination, point to weaknesses in all four management functions at once. For that reason the plan tackles them together. Planning gives people a clear direction through shared and agreed goals and a wider way of measuring results. Organising removes confusion by defining roles, deciding who makes which decisions, and building proper links between regions. Staffing makes sure that capable people are chosen, trained and treated fairly. Directing keeps people working well through flexible leadership, motivation that people believe in, and clear communication that respects cultural differences.

The advice rests on established ideas. Fayol supplies the basic structure. Drucker and Locke explain why clear goals improve performance. Mintzberg and Lawrence and Lorsch show why structure and coordination must suit complex, varied work. Hackman and Oldham and Herzberg connect job design and reward to motivation. Vroom, Hofstede and situational leadership guide how people should be led across countries. Many of these measures, such as named process owners and shared company goals, are already used by international firms.

If the plan is followed, I would expect decisions and delivery to become faster, less work to be repeated, and staff to be more engaged within about twelve to eighteen months, which the measures above would show. The plan does have limits. It rests on guesses about the causes that must first be checked on the ground. It needs steady support from senior leaders and money for training and systems. And because cultures differ, no single design will fit every office equally well, so the plan should be reviewed and adjusted as it goes. In the end, the main point is that effectiveness returns when direction, structure, people and leadership support one another instead of pulling in different directions.
