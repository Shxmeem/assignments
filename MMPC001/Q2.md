# A Management Action Plan to Restore Effectiveness in a Multinational Organisation

*Assignment: Planning, Organising, Staffing and Directing*

## 1. Introduction

Large companies that work in many countries often reach a point where things start to slip. The organisation in this case has reached that point. Employees are producing less now, many actually doesn't know what part their job covers and teams in different regions failing to work together.. These three problems feed each other. When a person does not know what they are responsible for, they either do too little or repeat what someone else is already doing. When two offices do not coordinate, work gets delayed or done twice. When people see their effort wasted, they stop trying as hard, and productivity falls further.

In this report as a outside management consultant My point is that the troubles are not because of lack of talent or anything else. It is a weakness in the basic everyday tasks of management. I have therefore organised the plan around the four functions that Henri Fayol (1916) described and that later writers such as Koontz and O'Donnell developed which are: planning, organising, staffing and directing. Controlling, the fifth function, is used at the end to check whether the plan is working. The four functions match the problems well. Unclear priorities are a planning issue. Unclear roles and poor coordination are organising issues. Skill gaps and poor fit are staffing issues. Low motivation and poor communication are directing issues.

Each recommendation is supported by a well-known theory or by common practice in international firms. The report begins with a short diagnosis of the likely causes. It then sets out actions under each of the four functions, followed by a short note on how to put the plan into effect and how to measure it. The conclusion pulls the points together and admits the limits of the advice. Since the case gives few facts, I assume that the company has several regional offices in different time zones, reports to a head office, and has no single cause for its problems.

## 2. Diagnosis and Action Plan

### 2.1 Diagnosis of the Problem

Before any action is taken, the actual causes of the problem need to be confirmed. This can be done through staff surveys, interviews with regional managers, and a review of data on output, delays and rework. Based on the symptoms described in the case, four causes are most likely. First, the goals of the head office may not be reaching employees in a clear form, so each region sets its own priorities. Second, reporting lines may be confusing, which is common in matrix structures where an employee reports to both a regional manager and a product manager (Galbraith, 2009). Third, coordination between regions may depend on emails and occasional calls instead of agreed routines, and differences in time zone, language and culture make this more difficult (Hofstede, 2001). Fourth, employees may be receiving little feedback or recognition, which reduces motivation over time.

### 2.2 Planning

Planning is the first step because roles and teamwork cannot be improved until employees know what the organisation is trying to achieve. Four actions are recommended.

The first action is to state the company's main objectives clearly and pass them down through every level using management by objectives (Drucker, 1954). Each region, team and individual would then have goals that link to the overall direction of the company. These goals should be agreed through discussion with employees and not simply imposed. According to Locke and Latham (1990), specific and reasonably challenging goals that people accept produce better performance than vague goals or goals that people feel are forced on them.

The second action is to measure performance on more than profit. The balanced scorecard of Kaplan and Norton (1992) covers financial results, customers, internal processes and learning. This prevents managers from chasing short-term output at the cost of skills that will be needed later.

The third action is to allow for local conditions. Lawrence and Lorsch (1967) found that units operating in different environments need different arrangements. A rule that works in Germany may not suit India or Brazil. The head office should therefore fix the objectives and the standards that cannot change, and allow each region to decide how to achieve them.

The fourth action is to use one planning calendar for all regions, with common dates for yearly, quarterly and monthly reviews. Without it, regions plan on separate timetables and it becomes difficult to see how their work fits together.

### 2.3 Organising

Organising deals directly with role ambiguity and poor coordination. The first recommendation is a review of all major roles. Each job description should state what the employee is responsible for, which decisions they can make and who they report to. Fayol's principle of unity of command, which says an employee should receive orders from one main superior, applies here. If a matrix structure must be kept, one reporting line should be named as the main one and the other as a supporting line.

Second, a responsibility chart such as RACI should be prepared for important processes. It shows who carries out the work, who is finally accountable, who must be consulted and who only needs to be informed. This removes uncertainty over who decides, which is a major cause of delay in large international firms.

Third, jobs should be designed carefully and not only described. Hackman and Oldham (1976) argued that employees perform better when a job is a whole piece of work, when its importance is visible, and when the employee has some freedom in how it is done.

Fourth, coordination should be planned. Mintzberg (1979) identified several ways of coordinating work, ranging from written rules and standard procedures to direct contact between people. Complex international work cannot depend on rules alone. Liaison staff between regions, cross-regional project teams and a shared online space for plans and documents are therefore recommended. A short written handover at the end of each working day, passing work from one time zone to the next, can also reduce delays. Decisions on brand, legal compliance and strategy should remain at the centre, while routine operational decisions should be moved closer to customers.

### 2.4 Staffing

A good structure will not work if employees lack the necessary skills or are placed in unsuitable roles. A skills audit should first be carried out to identify gaps. The results can then guide decisions on hiring, transfers and training.

Perlmutter (1969) described three approaches to staffing in multinational firms: preferring people from the home country, preferring local people in each country, or choosing the best person regardless of nationality. The third, called the geocentric approach, is most suitable here because it helps build a global team and a pool of future leaders. Selection should be based on clear job requirements and structured interviews, and for overseas posts it should also test the ability to work in other cultures.

Training should cover job skills, working with remote colleagues, cross-cultural communication and basic leadership. Kirkpatrick's four levels of evaluation, which are reaction, learning, behaviour and results, can be used to check whether the training has actually changed performance. Performance appraisal should be linked to the agreed goals, use feedback from more than one source and be adjusted between regions, since some cultures rate people generously and others strictly.

Career paths and international assignments give employees a reason to stay and help knowledge move around the organisation. Pay should reflect the local market but remain fair when regions are compared. Herzberg (1959) argued that pay and job security only prevent dissatisfaction, whereas responsibility, achievement and growth are what motivate. Both need attention.

### 2.5 Directing

Directing turns plans into daily work through leadership, motivation and communication. Managers should adjust their style to the team. Under Hersey and Blanchard's situational leadership, new or struggling teams need clear instructions and close support, while experienced teams need more freedom. Managers of remote teams will need training in this, since they cannot observe their staff directly.

Vroom's (1964) expectancy theory provides a practical basis for motivation. It states that people work harder when they believe effort leads to good performance, good performance leads to a reward, and the reward is valued. Managers can act on each link by giving clear goals and adequate resources, recognising results openly and offering rewards that employees actually value. Adams's (1963) equity theory adds that people compare their treatment with others, so rewards must also appear fair between offices.

Communication needs clear rules. Daft and Lengel's media richness theory suggests that complex or sensitive matters should be handled through richer channels such as video calls, while routine updates can be sent by email. The company should set norms for response times, rotate meeting times so that one region is not always working at night, and name who should be approached when an issue is stuck. Trust can be built through occasional face-to-face meetings, a proper induction for new members and a climate in which problems can be raised without fear (Edmondson, 1999). Managers must also remember that attitudes to authority and feedback differ between cultures (Hofstede, 2001), so one style cannot be applied everywhere.

### 2.6 Implementation and Control

The plan should be carried out in three stages. In the first three months, the most important roles should be clarified, communication rules agreed and decision charts prepared for the main processes. In the next six months, reporting lines should be changed, goals passed down and training started. After that, the plan should be reviewed and adjusted regularly. A steering group with a representative from each region can monitor progress. Suitable measures include output per employee, delays and rework, survey results on role clarity and engagement, the time taken by cross-regional projects, and staff turnover. Resistance to change should be expected. Kotter (1996) recommends explaining clearly why change is needed, securing visible support from senior leaders and showing early successes.

## 3. Conclusion

The problems of falling productivity, unclear roles and poor coordination show weaknesses in all four management functions at the same time, so the plan addresses them together. Planning gives employees a clear direction through agreed goals and a wider way of measuring results. Organising removes confusion by defining roles, deciding who makes which decisions and building proper links between regions. Staffing ensures that capable people are selected, trained and treated fairly. Directing keeps people working well through flexible leadership, motivation they believe in and clear communication that respects cultural differences.

The recommendations are supported by established theory. Fayol provides the basic structure. Drucker and Locke explain why clear goals improve performance. Mintzberg and Lawrence and Lorsch show why structure and coordination must suit complex work. Hackman and Oldham and Herzberg link job design and rewards to motivation, and Vroom, Hofstede and situational leadership guide how people should be led across countries. Several of these measures, such as named process owners and shared company goals, are already used by international firms.

If the plan is followed, faster decisions, less repeated work and higher engagement can be expected within about twelve to eighteen months, and the measures above would show this. The plan also has limitations. It depends on assumptions about the causes of the problem, which must first be confirmed. It requires steady support from senior leaders and investment in training and systems. Cultures differ as well, so no single design will suit every office equally well, and the plan should be reviewed and changed as it develops. Overall, effectiveness is restored when direction, structure, people and leadership support each other and do not pull in different directions.
