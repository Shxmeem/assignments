# Introduction

Marketing is the work of understanding buyers, offering them something useful and telling them about it honestly. These notes cover four ideas from the subject: how consumers decide, how products age, how messages are coordinated and how firms stay fair.

## a) Buying Decision Process

The buying decision process describes the steps a consumer takes from noticing a need to judging the purchase afterwards. Marketers study it because each step offers a chance to influence the buyer. The usual model has five stages.

The first is need recognition, when a person feels a gap between the present situation and the desired one, such as a phone that keeps hanging. The second is information search, where the buyer asks friends, reads reviews and compares prices online. The third is evaluation of alternatives, in which features, price, brand and after-sales service are weighed. The fourth is the purchase decision, which can still change because of a stock shortage or a delivery charge. The fifth is post-purchase behaviour. A satisfied customer may buy again and recommend the product, while a doubtful one may return it or complain.

The effort spent differs by product. Buying salt is nearly automatic, but buying a house or a car involves a long search. A marketer who knows where the customer is in the process can offer the right help, such as clear information early on or an easy return policy at the end.

## b) Product Life Cycle (PLC)

The product life cycle follows a product's sales and profit from its launch until it leaves the market. It has four stages.

In the introduction stage sales are low, because few people know the product, and profits are usually negative owing to heavy spending on promotion and distribution. In the growth stage sales rise quickly, profits improve and competitors enter. Electric cars in India are somewhere in this stage, with more models arriving each year.

In the maturity stage sales reach their highest level and then flatten, as most buyers already own the product. Competition is strong and prices come under pressure. Biscuits such as Parle-G are in this stage, and the company holds its position through steady quality, attractive packs and wide availability. In the decline stage, sales fall because tastes change or better products appear. Landline telephones are a familiar case, and firms may then cut costs, reduce the range or withdraw the product.

Not every product follows the same curve, but the model reminds managers that marketing plans must change as the product ages.

## c) Integrated Marketing Communication (IMC)

Integrated marketing communication means planning all the ways a company talks to its customers so that they deliver one clear and consistent message. These ways include advertising, personal selling, sales promotion, public relations, direct marketing and digital media. Without coordination, a company might promise luxury in its advertisements and offer constant discounts in its shops. Customers then become unsure what the brand stands for.

IMC begins with the customer, not the medium. The company decides what it wants buyers to know and feel, then selects the channels that reach them best and uses the same theme, tone and slogan in all of them. The result is a stronger brand image and less wasted money, because each effort supports the others.

Tanishq's festival and wedding campaigns offer an example. Television films, social media posts, shop displays and staff behaviour all speak of trust and family celebration, so the customer meets the same idea at every point.

## d) Ethical Marketing Practices

Ethical marketing means conducting marketing activities honestly and fairly, with regard for customers, competitors and society. It goes beyond what the law requires, though the law sets the minimum.

Common ethical issues include misleading advertisements that exaggerate benefits or hide conditions, unsafe products, unfair pricing, such as raising prices sharply during a shortage, misuse of customers' personal data, and advertising aimed at children. Another concern is greenwashing, where a firm claims a product is eco-friendly without evidence. In India the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 deals with misleading advertisements, and the Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) issues guidelines and handles complaints.

Practising ethical marketing means telling the truth about the product, stating prices and conditions clearly, protecting customer information and handling complaints fairly. It also pays commercially, because customers who feel treated honestly return and recommend the brand, while a company caught cheating loses trust that takes years to rebuild.

## Conclusion

The four topics work together. Knowing how buyers decide guides what a firm says, the life cycle shows how to adjust as the product ages, IMC keeps the message steady, and ethical practice protects the trust that all the rest depends on.
