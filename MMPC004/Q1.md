# Introduction

A businessman who does not keep proper accounts is like a traveller without a map. He may be busy and may even be earning money, but he cannot say how much he has earned, how much he owes or whether the business is moving in the right direction. **Accounting solves this problem.** It is the process of recording, classifying and summarising money transactions of a business and then reading the results. This assignment explains how accounting helps in:

- keeping systematic records,
- finding profit,
- understanding financial position, and
- making decisions.

---

## Maintaining Systematic Records

Every business has many transactions in a day, such as sales, purchases, expenses, payments to suppliers and money received from customers. Without a written record, it is easy to forget some of them or record them wrongly. **Accounting gives a fixed method for this work.**

1. **First**, each transaction is entered in the books in the order in which it happens, with its date, amount and details. This is done in a book called the *journal*, or in separate books for cash, sales and purchases.
2. **Next**, the entries are sorted into accounts, such as the cash account, the sales account, the rent account and the account of each customer and supplier. This is called the *ledger*.
3. **At the end of a period**, the balances of all accounts are listed in a *trial balance*, which also helps to check whether the entries are arithmetically correct.

This method brings several benefits:

- Nothing is left out or forgotten.
- The businessman can see at any time how much a customer owes him and how much he owes a supplier.
- Mistakes and cases of theft or fraud are easier to find because there is a record to check against.
- The records also serve as proof in a dispute, and they are needed for filing tax returns and for getting a bank loan.

---

## Determining Profit

The main aim of a business is to earn profit, but profit cannot be known by looking at the cash in hand alone. Cash may be high because the owner borrowed money, or low because stock was bought in bulk. **Accounting finds profit by comparing income with the expenses of the same period.**

At the end of the year, a *trading account* is prepared. It compares the sales with the cost of the goods sold, which gives the **gross profit**.

> **Example:** If goods that cost 60,000 were sold for 90,000, the gross profit is **30,000**.

After that, a *profit and loss account* is prepared. From the gross profit, the other expenses such as rent, salaries, electricity, transport and interest are subtracted, and any other income is added. What remains is the **net profit**. If the expenses are higher than the income, the result is a **net loss**.

Having this figure shows whether the business is making money at all. It also shows where the money is going. If the expenses on transport have risen sharply, or if the gross profit is falling while sales are rising, the businessman can see the problem and deal with it. The profit figure is also the base for working out income tax and for deciding how much the owner can safely withdraw.

## Assessing Financial Position

Profit tells how well the business performed during the year, but the owner also needs to know where the business stands on a particular date. This is shown by the **balance sheet**.

The balance sheet lists what the business owns and what it owes:

| **Assets** (what it owns) | **Liabilities** (what it owes) |
|---|---|
| Cash | Loans |
| Bank balance | Amounts payable to suppliers |
| Stock of goods | Unpaid expenses |
| Money to be received from customers | |
| Furniture, machinery and buildings | |

The owner's own share in the business, called **capital**, is the difference between the two. The two sides of the balance sheet always agree, since the assets are paid for either by the owner's money or by borrowed money.

From the balance sheet, the businessman can learn several things:

- He can see whether the business has enough cash and other current assets to pay its short-term dues, which tells him whether it is safe from a cash shortage.
- He can see how much of the business is funded by borrowing, and whether the debt is too high.
- He can see how much money is locked up in stock or is stuck with customers who have not paid.
- He can also see whether his own capital has grown or shrunk over the year.

This gives a true picture of the strength of the business, which no amount of guessing can give.

## Facilitating Decision-Making

Accounting information is the base for most decisions in a business, and **good decisions need facts**.

- Comparing the figures of one year with those of earlier years shows whether sales, profit and expenses are rising or falling, and helps in planning for the next year.
- Comparing the results of different products or branches shows which ones earn well and which cause losses, so that the businessman can push the first and reduce or stop the second.
- The records show how much it costs to make or buy a product, and so help in fixing a selling price that gives a fair profit.
- They show how much credit is being given to customers and how long they take to pay, so that the businessman can tighten credit where needed.
- Knowing the cash position helps him decide whether he can buy more stock, expand, hire staff or buy a machine, and whether he needs to borrow.
- Banks and investors ask for accounts before giving a loan or putting money in, so proper accounts make it easier to raise funds.
- Accounts are also used to estimate future income and expenses, which helps in making a budget and spotting trouble early.

---

## Conclusion

Accounting gives a businessman a clear and orderly record of his transactions, shows him the profit or loss through the profit and loss account, shows the financial position through the balance sheet and gives him the facts he needs to decide.

> A businessman who has no proper records can only **guess** about his business, while one who keeps proper accounts can manage it with **confidence**.
