# How Accounting Helps a Businessman Understand His Business

## Introduction

A businessman who keeps no proper accounts usually knows only a few things: how much cash is in the drawer, roughly what he owes and what customers owe him. He cannot say whether he made a profit last year, which product earns the most or whether he can afford a loan. Memory is unreliable, and numbers kept in the head are easily forgotten or disputed. Accounting solves this problem. It is the process of recording, classifying and summarising money transactions, and then using the results to understand and manage the business.

## Maintaining Systematic Records

The first help accounting gives is a proper record of every transaction. Each sale, purchase, payment, receipt and expense is written down when it happens, with the date, amount, parties involved and reason.

**How it is done.**
- Every transaction is first entered in a book of original entry, such as the cash book, sales book or purchase book, along with supporting papers like bills, invoices and receipts.
- It is then posted to separate accounts in the ledger, such as the account of each customer, each supplier, sales, rent, salaries and so on. This groups similar transactions together, so the total for any item can be found at once.
- At the end of a period, a trial balance is prepared to check that the records are arithmetically correct. The double entry system, where every transaction has two equal sides (a debit and a credit), makes many errors easy to detect.

**What the businessman gains.**
- He no longer depends on memory. He can see exactly what happened on any date.
- He knows how much each customer owes him and how much he owes each supplier, so he can collect dues on time and avoid paying twice or missing a payment.
- Records act as proof in disputes with customers, suppliers, partners or employees, and are accepted as evidence by tax authorities and courts.
- Theft, waste and mistakes are easier to notice, because the cash and stock shown in the books can be compared with what is actually present.

## Determining Profit

Without records, a businessman may think he is earning well simply because there is cash in hand. But cash in hand is not profit. It may come from a loan, from customers' advance payments or from selling an asset. It may also hide large unpaid bills.

Accounting finds the true profit by preparing the **trading and profit and loss account** for a period, usually a year.

- **Trading account:** Sales are compared with the cost of goods sold (opening stock plus purchases and direct expenses, minus closing stock). The difference is the **gross profit**.
- **Profit and loss account:** From gross profit, the running expenses of the business, such as rent, salaries, electricity, advertising and depreciation, are deducted, and other incomes are added. The balance is the **net profit** (or net loss).

*Example:* A shopkeeper sold goods worth ₹5,00,000 in a year. The goods cost him ₹3,50,000. Gross profit is ₹1,50,000. His rent, salary, electricity and other expenses came to ₹90,000. Net profit is ₹60,000. Without accounts, he might have assumed he earned the whole ₹1,50,000, and would have drawn too much for personal use.

**What the businessman gains.**
- He knows whether the business is making a profit or a loss, and how much.
- He can compare profit of this year with last year to see if the business is improving.
- By studying the expenses, he can see which costs are too high and where he can save.
- He can calculate gross profit and net profit as percentages of sales, and use them to judge efficiency.
- Profit figures are needed for paying income tax, and for sharing profits fairly among partners.

## Assessing Financial Position

Profit shows how the business performed during the year. Financial position shows where the business stands on a particular date, meaning what it owns and what it owes. Accounting shows this through the **balance sheet**.

- **Assets** are what the business owns or is owed: cash, bank balance, stock, money due from customers, furniture, machinery, land and buildings.
- **Liabilities** are what the business owes to others: money due to suppliers, bank loans and outstanding expenses.
- **Capital** (owner's equity) is what remains for the owner after liabilities are paid. It equals assets minus liabilities.

The balance sheet is based on the equation: **Assets = Liabilities + Capital**.

**What the businessman gains.**
- He sees how much of the business really belongs to him and how much belongs to outsiders.
- He can judge whether he has enough cash and quickly convertible assets to pay his short-term debts. A business can be profitable on paper and still fail because it has no cash to pay bills on time.
- He can see if too much money is locked up in stock or in unpaid customer bills.
- He can see whether debts are growing too large compared to his own capital, which signals risk.
- Banks and lenders ask for a balance sheet before giving a loan, because it shows whether the business can repay. Without one, the businessman may be refused credit or pay higher interest.
- If he wants to sell the business or bring in a partner, the balance sheet helps fix a fair value.

Simple ratios taken from these statements, such as profit as a percentage of sales, the proportion of current assets to current liabilities and the time taken to collect money from customers, make the picture clearer. They allow him to compare his business with earlier years and with similar businesses.

## Facilitating Decision-Making

The records and statements are useful only if they help in making choices. Accounting supports decisions in many ways.

**1. Pricing.** Once he knows the full cost of a product, including a fair share of overhead costs, he can set a selling price that gives a reasonable profit and avoid selling at a loss.

**2. Choosing what to sell.** Records show which products or branches bring in the most profit and which bring losses. He can push the strong ones and drop or improve the weak ones.

**3. Controlling costs.** Comparing expenses month by month shows unusual rises. Budgets can be set and actual spending checked against them, so waste is caught early.

**4. Managing credit.** He can see how long customers take to pay and which ones delay payment. He can then decide to whom to give credit, how much and for how long, and when to stop supplying.

**5. Managing cash.** By looking at expected receipts and payments, he can plan ahead for periods when cash will be short and arrange funds in time, instead of facing a sudden crisis.

**6. Borrowing and investing.** Before taking a loan or buying new machinery, he can check whether the business can afford the repayments from its profits. Past results help him estimate whether an expansion will pay back.

**7. Planning for the future.** Past figures help him forecast sales, costs and profit for the next year and set realistic targets.

**8. Meeting legal duties.** Accounts are needed for income tax, GST and other returns. Proper books reduce the risk of penalties and make filing simpler.

## A Practical Note

Even a small businessman does not need complicated systems to begin. A daily cash book, a record of credit sales and purchases, a stock register and a monthly summary of income and expenses already give him much better control than nothing. As the business grows, he can move to full double entry books or accounting software, and engage an accountant for tax and annual statements.

## Conclusion

Without accounting, a businessman is running his business blindly. He cannot tell whether he is earning or losing, what the business is worth, who owes him or whom he owes. Accounting corrects this by recording every transaction systematically, finding the true profit through the profit and loss account, showing the financial position through the balance sheet and supplying the facts needed for decisions on pricing, costs, credit, borrowing and expansion. Proper accounts are not only a legal requirement. They are the main source of information that allows the owner to manage his business with confidence.
