# Cost–Volume–Profit Analysis, Break-Even Point and Margin of Safety

## Introduction

Every business has to know how much it must sell to cover its costs and how much profit it will earn beyond that. A manager who does not know this can set wrong prices, produce too much or too little, and take decisions that damage the business. **Cost–Volume–Profit analysis**, the **Break-Even Point** and the **Margin of Safety** are three connected tools that help answer these questions. They show how costs, sales volume and profit are related to one another. This assignment explains each of these concepts and then discusses how they help a manager in taking decisions.

---

## Cost–Volume–Profit Analysis

**Cost–Volume–Profit analysis**, usually called **CVP analysis**, studies how profit changes when the cost of production and the volume of sales change. It is based on the idea that some costs stay the same whatever the level of output, while other costs move with output.

Costs are divided into two kinds:

- **Fixed costs** do not change with the number of units made or sold in the short run. Rent, salaries of permanent staff and insurance are examples.
- **Variable costs** change in direct proportion to output. Raw materials, packing and sales commission are examples.

The basic figure used in CVP analysis is **contribution**. Contribution is the selling price per unit minus the variable cost per unit. It is the amount each unit gives towards paying the fixed costs and, after they are paid, towards profit.

> **Example:** If a product sells for Rs. 50 and its variable cost is Rs. 30, the contribution is **Rs. 20 per unit**.

The relationship can be written as:

```
Profit = (Contribution per unit × Units sold) − Fixed costs
```

The contribution can also be shown as a percentage of sales, known as the **profit-volume ratio** or **P/V ratio**.

> In the example above, it is 20 ÷ 50 = **40%**. This means that out of every rupee of sales, 40 paise is available to meet fixed costs and give profit.

CVP analysis makes certain assumptions:

- The selling price per unit and the variable cost per unit are taken as constant.
- Fixed costs are taken as constant within the range of activity considered.
- The sales mix is assumed to stay the same if more than one product is sold.

These assumptions do not hold perfectly in real life, but they make the analysis simple and useful for planning.

## Break-Even Point

The **Break-Even Point** is the level of sales at which the business makes neither profit nor loss. At this point, total contribution is exactly equal to total fixed costs. Below it the business suffers a loss, and above it the business earns a profit.

It can be found in units:

```
Break-Even Point (units) = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit
```

It can also be found in rupees of sales:

```
Break-Even Point (sales value) = Fixed costs ÷ P/V ratio
```

Take a business with fixed costs of **Rs. 2,00,000** per year, a selling price of **Rs. 50** per unit and a variable cost of **Rs. 30** per unit. The contribution per unit is **Rs. 20**.

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Break-Even Point in units | 2,00,000 ÷ 20 | **10,000 units** |
| Break-Even Point in sales value | 2,00,000 ÷ 40% | **Rs. 5,00,000** |

So the business must sell 10,000 units, or Rs. 5,00,000 worth of goods, just to cover its costs. The **10,001st unit** is the first one that adds to profit.

If the business wants a **target profit**, the same method is used. The required sales are found by adding the target profit to the fixed costs and dividing by the contribution per unit.

> If the business wants a profit of Rs. 40,000, the units needed are (2,00,000 + 40,000) ÷ 20 = **12,000 units**.

A **break-even chart** shows this visually. Sales and total cost lines are drawn against volume, and the point where they cross is the break-even point. The gap between the lines to the left of this point shows loss, and the gap to the right shows profit.

## Margin of Safety

The **Margin of Safety** is the difference between actual (or planned) sales and break-even sales. It tells how far sales can fall before the business starts making a loss.

```
Margin of Safety = Actual sales − Break-even sales
```

It is often expressed as a percentage:

```
Margin of Safety percentage = (Actual sales − Break-even sales) ÷ Actual sales × 100
```

Continuing the example, suppose the business actually sells **15,000 units**.

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Margin of Safety (units) | 15,000 − 10,000 | **5,000 units** |
| Margin of Safety (value) | — | **Rs. 2,50,000** |
| Margin of Safety (percentage) | 5,000 ÷ 15,000 × 100 | **33.33%** |

This means sales can fall by **one-third** before the business reaches the break-even point.

A **high margin of safety** means the business is in a comfortable position. A **low margin of safety** means even a small fall in sales can push the business into loss. There are two ways to improve a weak margin of safety:

- raise sales, or
- lower the break-even point by cutting fixed costs, reducing variable costs or increasing the selling price.

## Role in Managerial Decision-Making

These three tools are used by managers in many everyday decisions.

**Pricing decisions.** A manager can check how a change in selling price affects the break-even point and profit. If the price is reduced to attract more buyers, CVP analysis shows how many extra units must be sold to earn the same profit as before.

**Deciding the volume of production and sales.** The manager knows the minimum quantity that must be sold to avoid loss. This helps in setting sales targets and production plans. Target profit calculations show the sales needed to reach a desired profit.

**Choice of product mix.** When a business makes more than one product, the contribution of each product can be compared. The manager can give more effort and resources to the products that give a higher contribution per unit or a higher P/V ratio.

**Cost control.** Separating fixed and variable costs shows which costs can be reduced and what effect the reduction will have on the break-even point. Lowering fixed costs, for example, brings the break-even point down directly.

**Make or buy and special orders.** If a customer offers a bulk order at a lower price, the manager can compare the offered price with the variable cost. If the price is above variable cost and there is spare capacity, the order adds to contribution and may be accepted even though it is below the normal price.

**Expansion and new projects.** Before starting a new product, opening a new branch or buying a machine, the manager can calculate the break-even point and check whether expected sales are comfortably above it. This reduces the risk of a wrong investment.

**Judging risk.** The margin of safety tells the manager how secure the business is. A business with a thin margin may decide to avoid taking on more fixed costs, while a business with a wide margin can consider expansion with more confidence.

---

## Conclusion

Cost–Volume–Profit analysis explains how profit depends on costs and sales volume. The Break-Even Point tells the manager the sales needed to cover all costs, and the Margin of Safety shows how much room the business has before it begins to lose money.

> Together they give a clear picture of the business and help in decisions on **pricing**, **production**, **product mix**, **cost control**, **special orders** and **new investments**.

Their results depend on the assumptions made, so managers should use them along with other information, but they remain **simple and practical tools for planning and control**.
