# Introduction

Every manager wants to know a few simple things. How many units must we sell to avoid a loss? What will happen to profit if sales rise or fall? What if we change the price or the cost? **Cost-volume-profit analysis**, the **break-even point** and the **margin of safety** help answer these questions. This assignment explains each of these ideas and discusses how managers use them to take decisions.

---

## Cost-Volume-Profit Analysis

**Cost-volume-profit analysis**, often called **CVP analysis**, studies how costs, sales volume and selling price together affect profit. Its base is the way costs behave:

- **Fixed costs**, such as rent and salaries, stay the same when output changes.
- **Variable costs**, such as raw material, rise in step with output.
- **Selling price per unit** is taken as constant.

With these, the profit at any level of sales can be worked out.

The key idea in CVP is **contribution**. Contribution is the selling price minus the variable cost. It is the amount each unit gives towards covering the fixed costs and, once they are covered, towards profit. A related figure is the **profit-volume ratio**, which is contribution divided by sales, shown as a percentage. It shows how much of each rupee of sales is available to cover fixed costs and give profit.

> **Example used throughout:** A company sells a product at **100 per unit**. The variable cost is **60 per unit**, and the fixed costs for the year are **2,00,000**.
>
> - Contribution = 100 − 60 = **40 per unit**
> - Profit-volume ratio = 40 ÷ 100 = **40 percent**

The basic relation is simple: **profit equals contribution minus fixed costs**. If the company sells 7,000 units, the contribution is 7,000 × 40 = 2,80,000. After the fixed cost of 2,00,000, the profit is **80,000**.

CVP analysis rests on certain assumptions:

- costs can be divided clearly into fixed and variable,
- the selling price and the variable cost per unit do not change,
- the fixed costs stay the same within the range considered, and
- all that is made is sold.

In real life, these may not hold exactly, which limits the results.

## Break-Even Point

The **break-even point** is the level of sales at which the company makes neither profit nor loss. At this point, the total contribution is just enough to cover the fixed costs. Below it, the company has a loss. Above it, there is profit.

It is found by dividing the fixed costs by the contribution per unit:

> 2,00,000 ÷ 40 = **5,000 units**

To get it in rupees, the fixed costs are divided by the profit-volume ratio:

> 2,00,000 ÷ 0.40 = **5,00,000 of sales**

This can be checked. At 5,000 units, the contribution is 5,000 × 40 = 2,00,000, the same as the fixed costs, so the profit is **nil**.

The break-even point tells the manager the minimum the company must sell to survive. A **lower break-even point means less risk**, since profit begins early. A company can lower it by:

- reducing fixed costs,
- raising the selling price, or
- cutting the variable cost.

The calculation is simplest when the company sells one product.

---

## Margin of Safety

The **margin of safety** is the difference between the actual sales and the break-even sales. It shows how far sales can fall before the company starts making a loss. It can be shown in units, in rupees or as a percentage of actual sales.

In the example, actual sales are 7,000 units and break-even sales are 5,000 units:

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Margin of safety (units) | 2,000 units |
| Margin of safety (value) | 2,00,000 |
| Margin of safety (percentage) | 2,000 ÷ 7,000 ≈ **29 percent** |

This means that sales can fall by about **29 percent** before the company reaches the point of loss. The margin of safety can also be worked out as profit divided by the profit-volume ratio:

> 80,000 ÷ 0.40 = **2,00,000**

A **high margin of safety** means the business is in a strong position, while a **low one** means that even a small fall in sales can bring a loss. Managers also watch this figure over the months, since a falling margin is an early warning.

## Role in Managerial Decision-Making

1. **Deciding the sales needed.** Managers can find the sales required to reach a target profit. If the company wants a profit of 1,20,000, the units needed are the fixed cost plus the target profit, divided by the contribution per unit:

   > (2,00,000 + 1,20,000) ÷ 40 = 3,20,000 ÷ 40 = **8,000 units**

2. **Fixing and changing prices.** If the price is cut to 90, the contribution falls to 30, and the break-even point rises to about **6,667 units**. The manager can see whether the extra sales expected from a lower price are enough to make up for it.

3. **Judging the effect of cost changes.** If the fixed costs rise to 2,40,000 because of a new rent, the break-even point goes up to **6,000 units**. A change in raw material price can be tested in the same way.

4. **Choosing the product mix.** When the company makes several products, those with a higher contribution can be pushed, since they add more to profit for each unit sold.

5. **Deciding whether to make or buy, accept a special order or expand.** Contribution shows whether a special order at a lower price still adds to profit, as long as the price is above the variable cost. It also shows whether a new machine, which increases fixed cost, is worth it.

6. **Assessing risk and planning.** The break-even point and margin of safety show how risky the business is. A manager with a small margin of safety may try to increase sales or cut fixed costs. These figures also help in preparing budgets and in discussions with banks.

---

## Conclusion

CVP analysis shows how costs, volume and price decide profit. The break-even point gives the sales at which the company just covers its costs, and the margin of safety shows how far sales can fall before a loss begins.

> Together, they help managers to **set targets**, **fix prices**, **control costs** and **judge risk**, though their results depend on the assumptions behind them.
