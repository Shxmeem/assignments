# Human Resource Accounting: Measuring the Value of Human Resources

## Introduction

A company spends a large amount every year on recruiting people, training them and looking after their welfare. In normal accounting, almost all of this is treated as an **expense** and written off in the year it is paid. Machinery bought for the business is shown as an asset, but the employees who run that machinery do not appear in the balance sheet at all. This gives an incomplete picture, because the skill and experience of employees often decide how well a company does. **Human Resource Accounting**, or **HRA**, tries to fix this by treating people as a resource that has a value and a cost. This assignment explains how HRA measures the value of human resources and how it helps management in decision-making.

---

## Meaning of Human Resource Accounting

**Human Resource Accounting** is the process of identifying, measuring and reporting the cost and value of employees to the organisation. It treats money spent on acquiring and developing employees as an **investment** that gives benefits over several years, and not as a cost that ends in one year. The aim is to show the worth of the workforce in figures so that management and investors can see it.

---

## How HRA Measures the Value of Human Resources

There are two broad ways of measuring human resources:

- **Cost-based methods** — look at the money the company has spent on its people.
- **Value-based methods** — look at what the employees are expected to earn for the company.

### Cost-Based Methods

| Method | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Historical Cost Method** | The actual amounts spent on recruitment, selection, training and development are added up and shown as an asset. This amount is then written off over the period the employee is expected to stay. If an employee is expected to stay for five years, one-fifth of the cost is written off each year. If the employee leaves early, the unwritten balance is charged off at that time. |
| **Replacement Cost Method** | This asks what it would cost **today** to replace the employee with a person of similar skill and experience. It includes the cost of hiring, training and the time taken to reach the same level of work. It gives a more current figure than the historical cost. |
| **Opportunity Cost Method** | The value of an employee is based on what the person would earn in the **best alternative use**. When a skilled person is scarce, the value is higher. |

### Value-Based Methods

| Method | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Present Value of Future Earnings** | The salary and benefits an employee is expected to receive until retirement are estimated and brought to their present value using a discount rate. The result is taken as the value of that person. This method was suggested by **Lev and Schwartz**. |
| **Economic Value Method** | The future contribution of a group of employees to the company's profit is estimated and discounted to the present. This ties the worth of employees to the results they produce. |

> **Note:** Cost methods are easier to apply because they use actual figures. Value methods are closer to the real worth of people, but they depend on estimates about the future, such as how long a person will stay and what discount rate to use.

---

## How HRA Assists Management in Decision-Making

1. **Recruitment and training decisions.** Because the cost of hiring and training is recorded, management can see how much each employee has cost and how long the benefit is expected to last. It can then judge whether a training programme or a recruitment drive is worth its cost.

2. **Decisions on retention.** When an employee leaves, the company loses the money invested in that person and also has to spend again on a replacement. HRA puts a figure on this loss. This helps management decide how much it is worth spending on pay, welfare and other measures to keep good employees.

3. **Placement and transfer.** The value of employees can be compared with their present jobs. If a person's value is higher than the work given to them, management may move that person to a role where the skills are better used.

4. **Planning the workforce.** HRA shows the strength and quality of the human resources the company has. Management can use this to decide how many people are needed, what skills are missing and what it will cost to fill the gap.

5. **Fixing pay and benefits.** Knowing the value of different groups of employees gives management a basis for deciding salaries, promotions and welfare spending in a fair way.

6. **Comparing choices.** Management often has to choose between buying a machine and hiring more people, or between training existing staff and recruiting new ones. HRA gives figures for the human side of these choices so that they can be compared with the cost of other options.

7. **Information for investors and others.** When the value of human resources is reported, investors, lenders and others can see the true strength of the company. It also shows employees that the company regards them as an asset, which can improve their morale.

8. **Controlling the use of people.** By following the cost and value of human resources over the years, management can find departments where labour cost is rising without matching returns. It can then take steps to correct this.

---

## Limitations

HRA is not free from problems:

- People are **not owned** by the company and can leave at any time, so it is hard to treat them as assets in the strict sense.
- Value-based methods depend on **estimates** of future service and earnings, which may turn out to be wrong.
- There is **no single method** accepted everywhere, and accounting standards do not require companies to show human resources in the balance sheet.

> These points mean the figures should be used with care.

---

## Conclusion

Human Resource Accounting treats the money spent on recruitment, training and welfare as an **investment** and tries to put a value on the people who make up the company. Cost-based and value-based methods are used for this, each with its own strengths and weaknesses.

> The figures help management decide on **hiring**, **training**, **retention**, **placement**, **pay** and **workforce planning**, and they give investors a better view of the company.

Although the methods rely on estimates and are not required in standard accounts, HRA gives management useful information about a resource that normal accounting leaves out.
