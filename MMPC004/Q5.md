# Introduction

In most companies, the money spent on hiring people, training them and looking after their welfare is treated as an ordinary expense and written off in the same year. Yet the people hired and trained keep working for the company for many years, much like a machine does. **Human Resource Accounting** tries to deal with this gap. It treats the spending on people as an **investment** and attempts to show the value of the workforce in numbers. This assignment explains how it measures the value of human resources and how it helps management in making decisions.

---

## Meaning of Human Resource Accounting

**Human Resource Accounting** is the process of identifying, measuring and reporting the cost and value of the people in an organisation. It treats employees as a resource that has value and that can grow or shrink, and not only as a cost of running the business.

Its main aims are:

- to show the investment made in people,
- to measure their worth to the company, and
- to give management information on how well this resource is being used.

---

## How Human Resource Accounting Measures the Value of Human Resources

There are two broad ways of measuring:

- One looks at **what the company has spent** on its people.
- The other tries to measure **what the people are worth** to the company.

### A. Cost Based Methods

1. **Historical Cost Method.** The company adds up the amount actually spent on recruiting, selecting, hiring, training and developing employees. This amount is shown as an asset and written off over the time the employee is expected to serve, much like the depreciation of a machine.

   > **Example:** If a company spends 3,00,000 on recruiting and training a group of engineers who are expected to stay for five years, it can treat 60,000 as the yearly cost. If an employee leaves early, the unwritten part is charged as a loss.

   The method is simple and based on facts, but it shows only what was spent and not what the employee is worth.

2. **Replacement Cost Method.** This asks what it would cost **today** to replace an employee with a similar one, including the cost of advertising, selection, training and the time lost until the new person works at the same level. It gives a better idea of present value than historical cost, but it is hard to find a person who is exactly equal.

3. **Opportunity Cost Method.** The value of an employee is judged by what he would earn in the best alternative use.

   > **Example:** If a manager is moved to a task where he adds less value, the loss is the value he would have added in his earlier post.

   Since a worker's value is high only if he is scarce, this method is used for scarce skills.

### B. Value Based Methods

1. **Present Value of Future Earnings.** The company estimates what an employee will earn, in salary and benefits, until retirement, and then brings these future amounts to their present value using a suitable discount rate. A person's worth is taken as this figure.

   > **Example:** If a person is expected to earn 6,00,000 a year for the next ten years, the total will be adjusted downward to show that money received later is worth less today.

2. **Economic Value of Contribution.** Here the value of a group of employees is taken as the extra profit they bring over what an average group would earn. A team that brings more profit than the usual, with the same capital, is worth more. Another idea, based on the chance that an employee will move to a new role or leave, assigns a value to each role and estimates how long a person is likely to stay in it.

> **Note:** Both types have uses. The cost methods are more reliable and the value methods show the real worth, though they depend on estimates.

## How Human Resource Accounting Assists Management in Decision-Making

1. **Recruitment decisions.** When the company knows the cost of recruiting and training someone and the value he brings, it can decide how many people to hire and which sources of hiring give the best return. If employees from one source stay longer and perform better, the company can recruit more from there.

2. **Training and development decisions.** Management can compare the cost of a training programme with the improvement in the value of employees. A programme that costs a lot but adds little can be changed or stopped, while one with a high return can be increased.

3. **Retention decisions.** When an employee leaves, the company loses the money spent on him and the value he would have added. Human resource accounting puts a figure on this loss. If the cost of losing a skilled worker is high, the company may decide to spend on raising pay, benefits or career growth to keep him.

4. **Promotion, transfer and placement.** Comparing the value of employees in different roles helps in placing people where they add the most. It gives a fairer base for promotion and transfers.

5. **Layoffs and downsizing.** Before reducing staff, management can see how much has been invested in the people concerned and what they would cost to replace when business improves. This can show that keeping a trained worker through a slow period is cheaper than hiring again later.

6. **Planning the workforce and budgeting.** Information on the size and value of the workforce helps in forecasting future needs, planning the cost of people and judging whether the company has the skills for its plans.

7. **Fairer view for investors and others.** A true picture of the company's assets, including people, helps investors, especially in service and knowledge based businesses where employees are the main source of value. It also shows employees that the company values them, which can raise their morale.

---

## Conclusion

Human Resource Accounting treats spending on people as an **investment** and gives it a value, either by measuring what has been spent or by estimating what people are worth.

> This information helps management to decide on **hiring**, **training**, **keeping employees**, **placing them in the right roles** and **planning the workforce**, so that the large sums spent on people are used with care.
