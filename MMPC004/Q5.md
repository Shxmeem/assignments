# Human Resource Accounting: Measuring the Value of People

## Introduction

A company that spends heavily on recruiting, training and looking after its employees is investing in them in the same way it invests in machinery. Yet in ordinary accounting, a machine bought for ₹10 lakh appears on the balance sheet as an asset, while ₹10 lakh spent on training is written off as an expense in the year it is spent. The result is odd: the books show training as a cost with no benefit, although the company expects the trained employees to produce results for years. Human Resource Accounting (HRA) tries to fix this by treating people as a resource with a measurable value.

## Meaning of Human Resource Accounting

Human Resource Accounting is the process of identifying, measuring and reporting information about the cost and value of an organisation's people, so that management and outsiders can use it. It does not mean that employees are owned like property. It means that the money spent on developing people, and the benefits expected from them, are recorded and analysed instead of being ignored.

## Why It Is Needed

- Traditional accounts show only physical and financial assets, though in many companies, especially in services and technology, people are the main source of income.
- Spending on people is treated as an expense, which lowers current profit and gives a false picture of both cost and return.
- Two companies with equal profit may have very different workforces, one strong and growing, the other tired and losing its best people. Ordinary accounts cannot show this.
- Management has no figures to judge whether training and welfare spending is worthwhile.

## Methods of Measuring the Value of Human Resources

There are two broad groups of methods: those based on cost and those based on value.

### A. Cost-based methods

**1. Historical cost method.** The company adds up what it actually spent on acquiring and developing employees: recruitment, selection, hiring, induction and training. This total is treated as an investment and is written off gradually over the period the employees are expected to stay, instead of being charged at once.

*Example:* Recruitment ₹50,000, selection ₹20,000, training ₹80,000 and induction ₹10,000 give a total of ₹1,60,000 for an employee expected to stay four years. Each year, ₹40,000 is charged as expense, and the unwritten balance appears as an asset. After one year, the balance is ₹1,20,000.

**2. Replacement cost method.** This asks what it would cost today to replace the present employee with an equally capable person, including recruitment, training and the loss from lower output while the new person learns. It shows the cost the company would face if key people left.

**3. Opportunity cost method.** This values an employee by what the person would earn in the best alternative use, for example, in another department. It is used where people are scarce and in demand.

### B. Value-based methods

**1. Present value of future earnings (Lev and Schwartz model).** The value of an employee is the present value of the earnings he or she is expected to receive over the working life, discounted at a suitable rate. It assumes that earnings reflect the value of the person's contribution.

*Example:* An employee will earn ₹6 lakh a year for five more years. At a 10% discount rate, the present value factor for five years is about 3.79. The value is 6 × 3.79 = about ₹22.7 lakh.

**2. Economic value method (Flamholtz).** This estimates the value of a person by the services he or she is likely to give in different roles, and the probability of staying in each. It considers movement, promotion and leaving, so it is closer to reality, but is more complex.

**3. Contribution-based approaches.** Some companies estimate the part of total profit that is due to the human element, by comparing their earnings with the industry average and attributing the extra to the quality of their people. The result is then discounted to find a total value for the workforce.

In practice, cost-based methods are easier and are better supported by records. Value-based methods give a more meaningful picture but depend on assumptions about future earnings, staying period and discount rates.

## How HRA Assists Management in Decision-Making

**1. Recruitment and selection.** HRA shows the full cost of hiring, which includes advertising, interviews, joining costs and the loss during the early period. If this cost is high, management has more reason to be careful in selection and to try cheaper channels such as employee referrals.

**2. Training and development.** By comparing the cost of training with the improvement in output or the savings that follow, management can see which programmes pay off and which do not. It also shows whether it is better to train existing staff or hire already skilled people.

**3. Retention and turnover.** When an employee leaves, the unwritten balance of the investment is lost and the replacement cost must be spent again. Putting a rupee figure on this makes the true cost of turnover visible, and justifies spending on better pay, welfare and career paths for those who are hard to replace.

**4. Allocation and transfer of people.** Management can compare the value of employees in different departments and place people where their value to the company is highest. It can also judge the effect of transferring or promoting someone.

**5. Planning the workforce.** Figures on the value and cost of the present workforce, along with age and experience, help forecast needs, plan succession and decide how much to invest in building skills for the future.

**6. Layoffs and restructuring.** Before reducing staff, management can compare the saving in wages with the loss of the investment made in those employees and the cost of rehiring later, so the decision is not based on salary savings alone.

**7. Judging managers and HR performance.** If managers are made responsible for the human assets under their charge, they are encouraged to look after their people and not just to meet short-term output targets. A manager who improves output by overworking and losing staff would show a fall in human asset value.

**8. Welfare spending.** HRA helps judge whether spending on health, housing, canteens or education for employees is producing returns in lower absence, fewer accidents and longer service.

**9. Mergers and acquisitions.** When buying another company, the value of its people, including key technical or management staff, can be a major part of what is being bought. HRA gives a basis for judging this.

**10. Information for outsiders.** Investors and lenders can use human resource information to judge the strength and stability of the company beyond what the usual accounts show. This is especially relevant for knowledge-based and service firms.

## Limitations and Criticisms

- **No agreed method.** Different methods give very different results, and companies cannot be compared fairly unless they use the same one.
- **Uncertainty.** Values depend on guesses about how long people will stay, their future earnings and the discount rate.
- **Employees are not owned.** People can leave at any time and cannot be bought or sold, so treating them as assets is questioned on both practical and ethical grounds. Some fear that it will lead to people being seen as objects.
- **Not recognised in accounting standards.** In India and most countries, accounting standards do not allow human resources to be shown as an asset on the balance sheet. Some companies, such as certain large public and software companies, have provided HRA as additional information outside the main statements, but this is voluntary.
- **Cost and effort.** Collecting the data is time-consuming and needs skill.
- **Risk of misuse.** Numbers may be used to justify decisions against employees, for example, to treat low-valued staff as unimportant.
- **Not all value is measured.** Qualities such as creativity, loyalty and teamwork are difficult to put into figures.

For these reasons, HRA is best used as an internal management tool alongside judgment, and not as an exact measure of what people are worth.

## Conclusion

A company that spends heavily on recruitment, training and welfare is making a long-term investment, but ordinary accounting hides this by charging it all as an expense. Human Resource Accounting corrects this by recording the cost of developing people and estimating their value, using cost-based methods such as historical and replacement cost, or value-based methods such as the present value of future earnings. The information helps management decide on hiring, training, retention, placement, restructuring and welfare spending with a clearer idea of costs and returns. Though HRA has limits, especially in measurement and recognition, it makes an important point: in many organisations, people are the most valuable resource, and a sound accounting system should at least try to show it.
