# Fixed, Variable and Semi-Variable Costs

## Introduction

Every business has costs, but they do not all behave in the same way. Suppose a bakery pays the same monthly rent whether it bakes 500 loaves or 5,000, yet its flour bill rises with every extra loaf. This difference in behaviour is the basis of cost classification by behaviour. Understanding it helps a company set prices, plan production and judge how much it must sell to avoid a loss. The statement in the question points to three types of cost: fixed, variable and semi-variable.

## Fixed Cost

A fixed cost is a cost that stays the same in total over a given period, whatever the level of production or sales, within a normal range of activity. The company must pay it even if nothing is produced.

**Examples:**
- Rent of the factory or shop
- Salaries of the manager, supervisors and office staff
- Insurance premium
- Depreciation of machinery, calculated by the straight-line method
- Interest on a loan
- Property tax

**Characteristics:**
- The total remains constant, but the cost **per unit falls** as production rises, because the same amount is spread over more units.
- It is linked to time, such as a month or a year, and not to output.
- It is fixed only within a "relevant range". If the company expands beyond its capacity, for example by renting a second factory, the fixed cost steps up to a new level. In the long run, nearly all costs can change.

*Example:* Factory rent is ₹50,000 a month. At 1,000 units, the rent per unit is ₹50. At 5,000 units, it is ₹10 per unit. The total rent has not changed at all.

## Variable Cost

A variable cost is a cost that changes in direct proportion to the level of production. If output doubles, the total variable cost doubles. If nothing is produced, the variable cost is zero.

**Examples:**
- Raw materials, such as cotton for a garment maker
- Wages of workers paid per piece produced
- Packing materials
- Sales commission calculated as a percentage of sales
- Fuel and power used directly in production
- Freight on goods sold

**Characteristics:**
- The total rises and falls with output.
- The cost **per unit remains constant**. If one shirt needs ₹200 of cloth, then every shirt needs ₹200, whether 10 or 10,000 are made.
- It is easier to control in the short term, since the company can reduce it by reducing production.

*Example:* If material cost is ₹40 per unit, then 1,000 units cost ₹40,000 and 5,000 units cost ₹2,00,000. The per-unit cost stays at ₹40.

## Semi-Variable Cost

A semi-variable cost, also called a mixed or semi-fixed cost, has two parts. One part is fixed and has to be paid even at zero activity. The other part is variable and increases as activity increases.

**Examples:**
- **Electricity bill:** a fixed monthly charge plus a charge per unit consumed.
- **Telephone or internet bill:** a fixed rental plus charges for calls or data beyond the plan.
- **Salesperson's pay:** a fixed salary plus commission on sales.
- **Machine maintenance:** a regular fixed servicing cost plus extra repairs that rise with heavy use.
- **Vehicle hire:** a fixed monthly rental plus a charge per kilometre.

**Characteristics:**
- The total cost never falls to zero, and it rises as output rises, but not in the same proportion as output.
- It can be written as: **Total cost = Fixed part + (Variable rate × Units)**.
- For decision-making, accountants usually split it into its fixed and variable parts, so that each can be treated properly.

*Example:* A factory's electricity bill is ₹5,000 fixed plus ₹4 per unit produced. At 0 units, the bill is ₹5,000. At 1,000 units it is ₹5,000 + ₹4,000 = ₹9,000. At 2,000 units it is ₹5,000 + ₹8,000 = ₹13,000. Output doubled from 1,000 to 2,000, but the bill rose only from ₹9,000 to ₹13,000, which is less than double.

## Comparison

| Basis | Fixed Cost | Variable Cost | Semi-Variable Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behaviour with output | Total stays constant | Total changes in proportion | Total changes, but less than in proportion |
| Cost per unit | Falls as output rises | Stays constant | Falls as output rises, but not as sharply as a fixed cost |
| At zero output | Still payable | Nil | Fixed part still payable |
| Depends on | Time period | Volume of activity | Both time and activity |
| Examples | Rent, insurance, manager's salary | Raw materials, piece-rate wages | Electricity, telephone, salesperson's salary and commission |

## A Combined Illustration

A company makes 1,000 units in a month. Its costs are:

- Rent (fixed): ₹20,000
- Materials (variable): ₹30 per unit
- Electricity: ₹3,000 fixed plus ₹2 per unit (semi-variable)

| Output | Rent | Materials | Electricity | **Total cost** | **Cost per unit** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 units | ₹20,000 | ₹0 | ₹3,000 | ₹23,000 | – |
| 1,000 units | ₹20,000 | ₹30,000 | ₹5,000 | ₹55,000 | ₹55.00 |
| 2,000 units | ₹20,000 | ₹60,000 | ₹7,000 | ₹87,000 | ₹43.50 |

When output doubles from 1,000 to 2,000 units, total cost rises from ₹55,000 to ₹87,000, which is an increase of about 58 per cent, not 100 per cent. The cost per unit falls from ₹55 to ₹43.50, because the fixed amounts are now shared over more units.

## Why This Classification Matters

- **Pricing:** A company must know its variable cost per unit to be sure that the selling price at least covers it, and its fixed costs to be sure that the overall price gives a profit.
- **Break-even analysis:** The number of units that must be sold to cover all costs is found by dividing fixed costs by the contribution per unit (selling price minus variable cost per unit). A company with high fixed costs needs to sell more to break even.
- **Cost control:** Fixed costs are controlled through decisions about capacity and commitments. Variable costs are controlled through efficient use of materials and labour.
- **Risk:** A business with a large share of fixed costs is more exposed when sales fall, because the costs remain. A business with mostly variable costs can adjust more easily.
- **Decisions on extra orders:** A company with spare capacity can often accept an additional order at a price above variable cost, since fixed costs are already being paid.
- **Budgeting and forecasting:** Splitting costs makes it possible to estimate total costs at different levels of production.

## Conclusion

Costs in a business behave differently as production changes. Fixed costs, such as rent and insurance, stay constant in total, so their cost per unit falls as output rises. Variable costs, such as raw materials and piece-rate wages, rise and fall in proportion to output, so the cost per unit stays the same. Semi-variable costs, such as electricity and telephone bills, contain both a fixed and a variable part, and change with output but less than proportionately. Separating the costs in this way gives management the information needed to price products, plan output, control spending and judge how much it must sell to make a profit.
