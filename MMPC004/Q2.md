# Introduction

When a company watches its costs for a few months, it soon notices that they do not all behave in the same way. The rent of the factory is the same whether the company makes 100 units or 10,000 units, while the bill for raw material goes up as more units are made. Some costs do both: they have a fixed part and a part that rises with output. Costs can therefore be grouped by how they react to the level of production. This assignment explains the three main groups:

- **fixed cost**,
- **variable cost**, and
- **semi-variable cost**,

with examples.

---

## Fixed Cost

A **fixed cost** is a cost that stays the same in total, within a certain range of production, whether the company produces more or less. It does not rise when output rises and does not fall when output falls. It has to be paid even if the factory is shut for a month and nothing is produced. If shown on a graph, a fixed cost is a **flat line parallel to the output axis**.

Examples include:

- rent of the factory building,
- salaries of the manager and office staff,
- insurance of the plant,
- depreciation of machinery calculated on the straight line method,
- interest on a loan, and
- licence fees.

Suppose a company pays rent of 50,000 a month. If it makes 1,000 units, the total rent is 50,000. If it makes 5,000 units, the total rent is still 50,000. The point to notice is that the **fixed cost per unit changes**.

> At 1,000 units, the rent per unit is **50**, but at 5,000 units it falls to **10**.

So the fixed cost is constant in total, but falls per unit as production rises. This is the reason why companies try to produce more, since the same fixed cost gets spread over more units.

Fixed costs are fixed only within a limit, called the **relevant range**. If production rises beyond what the present factory can handle, the company has to rent a second building or buy another machine, and the fixed cost steps up to a new level. Over a very long time, even these costs can change, but in the short run they are treated as fixed.

---

## Variable Cost

A **variable cost** is a cost that changes in direct proportion to the level of production. If the output doubles, the total variable cost doubles. If nothing is produced, the variable cost is zero.

Examples include:

- raw material,
- packing material,
- wages paid to workers on a piece rate basis (for each unit made),
- power used to run machines that depends on output, and
- sales commission paid on each unit sold.

Fuel for a delivery vehicle is another case, since it depends on how many trips are made. On a graph, variable cost is a **straight line that starts at zero and rises as output rises**.

Suppose a furniture maker spends 400 on wood and 100 on labour and finishing for each chair.

| Output (chairs) | Variable Cost |
|---|---|
| 100 | 50,000 |
| 200 | 1,00,000 |
| 500 | 2,50,000 |

The total grows with the output, but the **cost per chair stays the same at 500**. That is the opposite of fixed cost, which is constant in total and changes per unit.

Variable costs are easier to control in the short run because the company can reduce them by producing less. They matter in pricing, since the selling price must at least cover the variable cost of each unit, or the company loses money on each sale.

---

## Semi-Variable Cost

A **semi-variable cost**, also called a *mixed cost*, has two parts. One part is **fixed** and has to be paid even at zero output. The other part is **variable** and rises as production rises. The total is therefore never zero, but it also keeps going up as output goes up, though not in the same proportion. On a graph, it is a line that starts above zero, at the fixed part, and then rises. Such costs are common in real business, since few costs are purely fixed or purely variable.

Examples include:

- the electricity bill of a factory, which has a fixed minimum charge each month plus a rate for each unit of power used,
- the telephone bill, which has a fixed rental and then a charge for calls,
- repair and maintenance of machines, which has some regular servicing that is done anyway, and more repairs as the machines are used more,
- a salesperson who gets a fixed salary plus a commission on sales, and
- the rent of a vehicle, which may be a fixed monthly amount plus a charge for each kilometre.

Suppose the electricity bill has a fixed charge of 10,000 a month and 5 for each unit of power used.

| Power Used (units) | Bill |
|---|---|
| 2,000 | 10,000 + 10,000 = **20,000** |
| 4,000 | 10,000 + 20,000 = **30,000** |

The bill rose by half when the use doubled, since a part of it did not move.

For planning, accountants usually **split** such costs into their fixed and variable parts. The fixed part is added to fixed costs and the variable part is added to variable costs. This makes it easier to predict the cost at any level of production.

---

## Comparison of the Three Costs

| Cost Type | In Total | Per Unit | At Zero Production |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed** | Stays the same | Changes (falls as output rises) | Remains |
| **Variable** | Changes in proportion to output | Stays the same | Nil |
| **Semi-variable** | Changes, but less than in proportion | Changes | Equal only to its fixed part |

Take a company with a fixed cost of 50,000 and a variable cost of 500 per unit.

| Output (units) | Total Cost | Cost per Unit |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 1,00,000 | 1,000 |
| 1,000 | 5,50,000 | 550 |

The cost per unit falls because the fixed part is shared among more units.

---

## Conclusion

The statement that some costs remain constant while others change with output is explained by this classification.

- **Fixed costs**, such as rent and salaries, do not move with production.
- **Variable costs**, such as raw material and piece wages, move in step with it.
- **Semi-variable costs**, such as electricity and telephone bills, have a fixed part and a changing part.

> Understanding these helps a company to **plan**, **control its costs** and **decide on prices**.
